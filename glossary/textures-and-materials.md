# Textures & Materials

How surfaces look and feel in an image — the visual weight, tactile quality, and material identity of everything in the frame. In prompt composition, texture and material descriptors control whether a surface reads as luxurious or gritty, organic or synthetic, smooth or rough. They're the difference between "a wall" and "a crumbling limestone wall with peeling plaster."

---

## Surface Quality

The fundamental spectrum from matte to mirror. How light interacts with a surface determines how we read its material identity.

### Matte

**What it is**: A surface that scatters reflected light in all directions equally (diffuse reflection). No sheen, no glare, no reflections. Examples: raw concrete, chalk, unfinished wood, matte paper, matte skin (no oil/sweat).

**Visual effect**: Soft, quiet, unassuming. The surface absorbs attention rather than deflecting it. Colors appear more even across the surface since there are no bright specular highlights. Textures are visible but gentle. Reads as understated, organic, or deliberately controlled.

**Pairs well with**:
- Soft/diffused lighting — matte surfaces don't fight soft light
- Minimal/modern aesthetics — matte is the surface of quiet design
- Product photography with muted palettes — matte objects in matte light
- Scandinavian design — matte wood, linen, ceramic
- Flat lay compositions — matte surfaces don't create distracting reflections

**Conflicts with**:
- Glamour/luxury — matte is too quiet (unless deliberately understated luxury)
- Dramatic specular highlights — matte surfaces don't produce them
- Wet/reflective aesthetics — the opposite surface quality

**Example combination**: Matte ceramic + matte linen + soft diffused light + desaturated palette → quiet, tactile, understated, every surface absorbs light evenly

---

### Glossy / Polished

**What it is**: A smooth surface that produces specular reflections — clear, mirror-like bouncing of light. Examples: polished metal, wet surfaces, lacquered wood, glossy paper, glass, patent leather.

**Visual effect**: Bright specular highlights, clear reflections of surrounding environment, strong contrast between highlight and shadow on the surface. Reads as high-end, polished, luxurious, or wet. The highlights catch the eye and create visual energy.

**Pairs well with**:
- Hard/directional lighting — creates defined specular highlights
- Product photography — the polish signals quality and finish
- Luxury/fashion — glossy surfaces read as expensive
- Dark backgrounds — specular highlights pop against darkness
- Controlled studio light — precise highlights on precise surfaces

**Conflicts with**:
- Matte/organic aesthetics — too reflective
- Soft/diffused light — doesn't produce the strong highlights glossy needs
- Rustic/natural mood — too polished

**Example combination**: Glossy black lacquer surface + hard key light + product on top + dark background → sharp specular highlight streaking across the surface, product reflected, luxury and precision

---

### Satin / Semi-Gloss

**What it is**: Between matte and glossy — a soft sheen that produces diffused highlights rather than sharp reflections. Examples: silk fabric, brushed metal, satin-finish paint, healthy skin.

**Visual effect**: Soft glow rather than sharp reflection. Highlights are broader and gentler than on glossy surfaces. The surface catches light without creating distracting mirror reflections. Reads as refined, healthy, premium.

**Pairs well with**:
- Beauty/skin photography — healthy skin has a satin quality
- Soft directional light — produces broad, flattering highlights
- Fabric/textile focus — silk, satin, charmeuse have this quality
- Warm tones — satin highlights pick up light color beautifully

**Conflicts with**:
- Maximum reflectivity — satin is softer than full gloss
- Completely matte — satin has some sheen
- Gritty/rough textures — satin is smooth

---

### Metallic

**What it is**: The specific reflective quality of metals — high specular reflection combined with the material's own color tinting the reflection. Gold reflects warm, chrome reflects neutral, copper reflects warm-pink, brushed aluminum scatters.

**Visual effect**: Reflective but colored by the metal itself. Chrome mirrors the environment; gold warms everything it reflects; copper adds pink warmth. Metallic surfaces create some of the most complex light interactions in photography — reflections, caustics, color bleed onto adjacent surfaces.

**Pairs well with**:
- Controlled/studio lighting — metallic surfaces are notoriously difficult to light; controlled environments produce the best results
- Dark/moody backgrounds — metal pops against darkness
- Product photography — jewelry, watches, hardware, automotive
- Complementary color accents — gold against deep blue, chrome against red

**Conflicts with**:
- Flat/even lighting — metallic surfaces look dead without highlights
- Bright/cluttered backgrounds — reflections pick up everything
- Matte/organic palettes — metallic is inherently synthetic and shiny

---

### Translucent / Frosted

**What it is**: A surface that transmits light but scatters it — you can see light through it but not clear images. Examples: frosted glass, wax, thin paper, backlit leaves, alabaster, skin lit from behind (subsurface scattering).

**Visual effect**: Soft internal glow. Light passes through and diffuses, creating a luminous quality from within. The object appears to glow rather than reflect. Reads as delicate, ethereal, organic (when biological), or elegant (when architectural/designed).

**Pairs well with**:
- Backlighting — light from behind reveals the translucency
- Macro photography — thin materials become luminous at close range
- Nature — backlit leaves, petals, ice, thin shells
- Soft/warm light — the translucency diffuses and warms light beautifully
- Ethereal/dreamlike mood — the inner glow is inherently otherworldly

**Conflicts with**:
- Front lighting — translucency is invisible without backlight
- Opaque/heavy materials — can't be translucent
- Hard/gritty aesthetic — too delicate

**Example combination**: Backlit translucent leaves + macro + warm light + shallow depth of field → leaves glowing green-gold from within, veins visible as dark networks, soft bokeh behind, ethereal

---

### Transparent / Glass

**What it is**: Fully see-through materials — glass, crystal, clear water, clear plastic. Light passes through with minimal scattering. Refracts and bends what's behind it.

**Visual effect**: Distortion and refraction of background elements. Specular highlights on the surface. Caustic light patterns cast onto surfaces below. The material is defined more by what it does to light passing through it than by its own appearance.

**Pairs well with**:
- Hard/directional light — creates caustics and defined highlights
- Dark backgrounds — the glass edges and highlights become visible
- Product photography — perfume bottles, glassware, drinks
- Water as a subject — drops, splashes, surfaces

**Conflicts with**:
- Flat/even light — transparent objects disappear without directional light
- Matte/diffused aesthetics — glass is all about sharp light interaction
- Subjects that need to be clearly visible — glass distorts what's behind it

---

### Iridescent / Holographic

**What it is**: Surfaces that shift color depending on viewing angle (iridescent: soap bubbles, beetle shells, oil slicks) or display rainbow diffraction patterns (holographic: foil, stickers, packaging).

**Visual effect**: Rainbow color shifts, prismatic reflections, dynamic appearance implying movement even in stills. Reads as futuristic, magical, or precious. Iridescent is organic (pearl, shell); holographic is synthetic (foil, packaging).

**Pairs well with**: Y2K aesthetic, vaporwave, sci-fi, fashion editorial, dark backgrounds, macro photography.

**Conflicts with**: Muted/earthy palettes, matte textures, vintage aesthetics, warm color grading.

---

### Pearlescent

**What it is**: Soft lustrous sheen with subtle color shift — like the inside of an oyster shell. Less dramatic than iridescent. Shifts mainly between white and one or two soft colors.

**Visual effect**: Soft glow, elegant, subtle color play, luminous without flashy. Reads as luxury, feminine, delicate.

**Pairs well with**: Soft lighting, beauty photography, luxury branding, pastel palettes, wedding aesthetics, silk.

**Conflicts with**: Harsh contrast, gritty textures, industrial aesthetics, bold saturated color.

## Organic Textures

Natural, living, or once-living surfaces with irregular, non-repeating patterns.

### Skin

**What it is**: Human skin as a photographic surface — pores, fine lines, peach fuzz (vellus hair), oil/sweat sheen, freckles, moles, scars, and color variation.

**Visual effect**: The most scrutinized texture in photography. Hard light reveals every pore and imperfection. Soft light smooths and flatters. Oil/sweat creates specular highlights that read as youthful and healthy (or as unwanted shine, depending on context). Subsurface scattering in backlit skin creates a warm glow (light passing through ears, fingers, thin skin).

**Pairs well with**:
- Macro/ECU — pores become landscapes, freckles become features
- Soft beauty light — smooths texture, creates flattering satin sheen
- Hard editorial light — reveals everything, raw and honest
- Warm tones — healthy skin reads warm
- Portra film — designed specifically for skin tone rendering

**Conflicts with**:
- Extreme hard light + beauty standards — reveals "flaws" (though editorial embraces this)
- Heavy grain — competes with skin texture for detail
- Cool/desaturated tones — makes skin look unhealthy

---

### Wood

**What it is**: Grain patterns, knots, rings, and surface quality of wood — from raw/unfinished to polished/lacquered.

**Visual effect**: Warm, organic, natural. The grain pattern is always unique. Raw wood reads as rustic, handmade, honest. Polished wood reads as refined, warm luxury. Weathered/aged wood reads as history and character. The color ranges from pale birch to deep walnut.

**Pairs well with**:
- Warm lighting — amplifies wood's natural warmth
- Rustic/natural mood — raw wood is the material of craft
- Food/product styling — wood surfaces as backgrounds
- Hard side light — raking light reveals grain texture dramatically
- Matte or satin finish — the surface quality changes the read entirely

**Conflicts with**:
- High-tech/futuristic aesthetic — wood is natural and traditional
- Cool/clinical tones — fights the warmth
- Flat/front light — grain texture disappears without directional light

---

### Stone / Rock

**What it is**: Granite, marble, slate, sandstone, concrete, limestone — dense, heavy, ancient-feeling materials with varied surface qualities.

**Visual effect**: Solidity, permanence, weight. Rough stone reads as rugged and natural. Polished marble reads as luxurious and classical. Concrete reads as urban, industrial, modern. Each stone type has a distinct visual character — marble's veining, granite's speckle, slate's layers, sandstone's warmth.

**Pairs well with**:
- Hard directional light — reveals surface texture and dimensionality
- Architectural subjects — stone is the material of buildings
- Minimal compositions — a stone surface as a clean background
- Cool/neutral tones — stone is naturally neutral
- Product photography — marble and concrete as product surfaces

**Conflicts with**:
- Soft/delicate mood — stone is heavy and permanent
- Warm organic textures — stone is cold (marble, granite) or earthy (sandstone)
- Lightweight/airy feeling — stone has visual weight

---

### Fabric / Textile

**What it is**: The spectrum of woven, knitted, and felted materials — each with its own drape, weight, texture, and light interaction.

**Visual effect**: Depends entirely on the specific textile:
- **Silk/satin** — fluid drape, soft specular highlights, luxurious
- **Linen** — visible weave, matte, relaxed, natural
- **Velvet** — deep, light-absorbing, rich, directional nap
- **Denim** — sturdy, woven texture, casual, workwear
- **Leather** — heavy, textured, semi-gloss, rugged or refined
- **Lace** — intricate, delicate, semi-transparent pattern
- **Wool/knit** — soft, textured, warm, cozy
- **Chiffon/organza** — sheer, floating, ethereal

**Pairs well with**:
- Fashion photography — fabric IS the subject
- Soft directional light — reveals drape and fold
- Macro/detail shots — weave patterns become abstract
- The mood matching the textile — velvet for luxury, linen for casual, lace for delicacy

**Conflicts with**:
- Mismatched mood — velvet in a casual setting, denim in a formal one (unless deliberately subversive)
- Flat/front light — fabric drape needs side light to create shadows in the folds

---

### Water

**What it is**: As a surface/texture, not a subject — the reflective, refractive, moving qualities of water. Still water, rippled, splashing, droplets, condensation, wet surfaces.

**Visual effect**: 
- **Still water** — mirror reflections, doubled scene, calm
- **Rippled** — distorted reflections, movement, shimmer
- **Splashing** — frozen motion (fast shutter) or streaked motion (slow shutter), energy
- **Droplets** — macro-scale, refractive, each drop is a tiny lens
- **Wet surfaces** — adds specular reflection to any surface, darkens colors, adds glossy sheen
- **Condensation** — intimacy (steamy windows, cold glasses), obscuring detail

**Pairs well with**:
- Neon/artificial light + wet streets — reflections multiply light and color (the Blade Runner effect)
- Macro + water droplets — each drop becomes a tiny world
- Long exposure + flowing water — silky smooth texture
- Hard light + wet skin — specular highlights, athletic/dramatic

**Conflicts with**:
- Matte aesthetics — water adds gloss to everything
- Dry/arid mood — water changes the texture of every surface it touches
- Controlled studio work — water is unpredictable and messy

**Example combination**: Wet city street + neon signs + night + CineStill 800T → every light source doubled in puddle reflections, halation halos reflected, wet asphalt becomes a mirror of color

---

### Marble (Specific Veining Types)

**What it is**: The vein patterns in marble — each type has dramatically different character:
- **Carrara** — grey veining on white, subtle, classic Italian
- **Calacatta** — bold gold/grey veining on white, dramatic, expensive-looking
- **Nero Marquina** — white veining on black, dramatic
- **Verde Guatemala** — white veining on deep green
- **Rose marble** — warm pink with grey/white veining

**Visual effect**: Organic flowing lines, luxury, classical. Each type sets a different mood — Calacatta is bold, Carrara is subtle, Nero Marquina is dramatic.

**Pairs well with**: Luxury branding, classical architecture, flat lay photography, gold accents, minimalist design.

**Conflicts with**: Industrial/tech (usually), rustic/rough textures, busy patterns competing with the veining.

---

### Denim

**What it is**: Woven cotton twill with characteristic diagonal ribbing. Varies from raw/rigid (dark, uniform indigo) to heavily distressed (faded, torn, whiskers).

**Visual effect**: Raw denim reads as sharp/intentional; distressed reads as lived-in/rebellious. Casual, American, workwear heritage. The texture is universally recognizable.

**Pairs well with**: Americana, street photography, fashion editorial, warm natural light, leather and copper accents.

**Conflicts with**: Formal/luxury (unless deliberately subverted), high-tech surfaces, very polished textures.

---

### Leather (Specific Types)

- **Patent leather** — high-gloss lacquered mirror finish. Formal, fetish, fashion. Catches highlights sharply.
- **Suede** — napped, soft, matte. Warm, tactile, casual luxury. Absorbs light.
- **Distressed leather** — worn, cracked, faded. Rugged, vintage, authentic. Shows use and history.
- **Tooled/Embossed** — stamped decorative patterns. Western, artisan, ornate.
- **Vegetable-tanned** — natural tan, ages/patinas over time. Craft, heritage, honest.

Each type reads completely differently. Always specify which — "leather texture" alone defaults to generic smooth brown.

**Pairs well with**: Warm lighting, earth tones, period settings, close-up detail shots.

## Synthetic / Manufactured Textures

### Chrome / Mirror Finish

**What it is**: Maximum reflectivity — a perfect or near-perfect mirror surface. Chrome plating, polished stainless steel, mirrored glass.

**Visual effect**: The surface disappears — you see only the reflection of the environment. The object's shape is defined by how it distorts the reflected world. Reads as futuristic, industrial, high-end, or sci-fi.

**Pairs well with**:
- Controlled environments — reflections pick up EVERYTHING, so the environment must be intentional
- Studio/product photography — controlled reflections define the shape
- Futuristic/sci-fi aesthetic — chrome is the material of the future
- Dark environments with specific light sources — the reflections become graphic

**Conflicts with**:
- Cluttered environments — the chrome reflects the mess
- Natural/organic mood — chrome is entirely synthetic
- Flat lighting — chrome needs something interesting to reflect

---

### Plastic / Acrylic

**What it is**: Synthetic polymer surfaces — from matte to glossy, opaque to transparent. Includes acrylic, vinyl, resin, silicone, PVC.

**Visual effect**: Varies hugely by type, but the common thread is a synthetic quality: too uniform for natural, too smooth for organic. Glossy plastic has a bright, cheap-or-fun quality. Clear acrylic reads as modern and clean. Matte plastic reads as industrial or consumer-tech.

**Pairs well with**:
- Pop/commercial aesthetics — plastic is the material of consumer products
- Bright saturated colors — plastic takes dye well, colors are uniform and vivid
- Y2K / retro-futurism — transparent and colored plastic is a Y2K signature
- Product photography — electronics, toys, packaging

**Conflicts with**:
- Luxury/premium — plastic reads as cheap (unless deliberately ironic)
- Natural/organic aesthetic — plastic is the opposite of natural
- Vintage — plastic is modern (except retro-kitschy contexts)

---

### Concrete / Brutalist Surface

**What it is**: Raw, unfinished concrete — board-formed (showing wood grain imprint), smooth-poured, or rough-aggregate. The material of Brutalist architecture.

**Visual effect**: Raw, heavy, urban, imposing. The gray surface absorbs and scatters light, creating a flat but textured backdrop. Board-formed concrete shows the ghost of the wooden forms used to pour it — a texture-within-a-texture. Reads as industrial, institutional, or deliberately austere.

**Pairs well with**:
- Brutalist architecture — the material IS Brutalism
- Hard/directional light — raking light reveals the texture
- Minimal compositions — concrete as a clean but textured background
- Cool/desaturated tones — concrete's natural gray reinforces coolness
- Fashion/editorial contrast — human warmth against cold concrete

**Conflicts with**:
- Warm/cozy mood — concrete is cold and austere
- Natural/organic scenes — concrete is manufactured
- Polished/luxury aesthetics — unless brutalist-luxury

**Example combination**: Raw concrete wall + hard side light + fashion model + desaturated grade → texture revealed in raking light, warm skin against cold surface, industrial-editorial contrast

---

### Rust / Patina / Decay

**What it is**: Surface degradation — oxidized metal (rust), aged copper (verdigris), peeling paint, cracked plaster, weathered wood. The visible evidence of time and exposure.

**Visual effect**: Age, character, history, impermanence. Rich warm-orange rust, blue-green verdigris, flaking layers revealing what's underneath. The surface tells a story of time passing. Reads as industrial, post-apocalyptic, wabi-sabi, or authentically aged.

**Pairs well with**:
- Warm/directional light — reveals the color and texture depth
- Macro detail — the decay patterns are beautiful at close range
- Vintage/industrial aesthetics — the material of abandoned spaces
- Wabi-sabi philosophy — beauty in imperfection and impermanence
- Color accent — rust orange and verdigris green are vivid against neutral surroundings

**Conflicts with**:
- Clean/new aesthetics — decay is the opposite of pristine
- Modern/tech — rust signals neglect in a tech context
- Sterile/clinical environments — decay is organic and unpredictable

---

### Neon / LED Glow

**What it is**: Self-luminous surfaces — neon tubes, LED strips, backlit signs, screen glow. Not a texture in the traditional sense, but a surface quality that changes everything around it.

**Visual effect**: The surface emits light rather than reflecting it. Creates color wash on nearby surfaces. Defines zones of color in the scene. The glow bleeds outward, especially in fog, rain, or on reflective surfaces. Reads as urban, nightlife, futuristic, or cyberpunk.

**Pairs well with**:
- Nighttime/low light — neon is most effective in darkness
- Wet surfaces — reflections multiply the color
- Fog/haze — volumetric glow around the source
- Cyberpunk/sci-fi aesthetic — neon is the genre's material
- Portraiture — neon as colored key light on the face

**Conflicts with**:
- Natural/organic environments — neon is urban and synthetic
- Daytime — neon is invisible in bright ambient light
- Warm/natural skin tones — the color cast changes everything

---

### Brushed Metal

**What it is**: Metal finished with fine parallel abrasion lines, creating directional reflection rather than mirror reflection. Common on appliances, watches, industrial design.

**Visual effect**: Soft directional highlight streaks. Matte-adjacent but clearly metallic. Industrial sophistication. Visible grain and directionality in the surface. Reads as modern, industrial, premium.

**Pairs well with**: Industrial design, product photography, minimalist aesthetic, cool color temperatures, modern architecture.

**Conflicts with**: Organic/natural aesthetics, warm rustic looks, highly polished mirror surfaces.

---

### Carbon Fiber

**What it is**: Woven carbon filament composite with distinctive visible twill weave pattern. Used in automotive, aerospace, luxury goods.

**Visual effect**: Dark, almost black with subtle sheen. Visible geometric weave pattern catches light. Reads as high-tech, performance, expensive. The weave creates a subtle moiré-like texture.

**Pairs well with**: Automotive photography, tech products, dark/moody lighting, red + carbon fiber (classic combo), industrial luxury.

**Conflicts with**: Organic/natural subjects, soft/feminine aesthetics, vintage, pastels.

---

### Ceramic / Porcelain

**What it is**: Fired clay materials ranging from rough stoneware to ultra-smooth porcelain. **Ceramic** — handmade, warm, slightly imperfect. **Porcelain** — refined, cool, elegant, sometimes translucent. Glazed adds shine; unglazed (bisque) is matte and chalky.

**Visual effect**: Ceramic reads as artisan, wabi-sabi, craft. Porcelain reads as pristine, cool, luxury. The spectrum from rough to refined spans the entire material.

**Pairs well with**: Still life, minimalist design, food photography, Japanese aesthetics (wabi-sabi for rough ceramic), luxury branding (fine porcelain).

**Conflicts with**: Industrial/tech (for ceramic); rustic/rough (for fine porcelain).

---

## Paper & Foil

### Paper Textures

- **Kraft** — brown, unbleached, rough. Eco, packaging, rustic, DIY. Visible fiber.
- **Vellum** — smooth, translucent, slightly waxy. Classical, archival, precious.
- **Handmade / Deckle edge** — irregular edges, visible fiber, uneven surface. Artisan, letterpress, fine art.
- **Japanese washi** — translucent, fibrous, delicate. Long visible fibers. Japanese aesthetic, meditation, light-filtering.
- **Cotton rag** — thick, textured, archival. Fine art, printmaking, photography printing.

Each paper type completely changes the mood of anything printed on or placed against it.

**Pairs well with**: Typography, letterpress, stationery design, botanical illustration, warm soft lighting.

**Conflicts with**: Digital/screen aesthetics, high-gloss finishes, high-tech subjects.

---

### Foil / Leaf

- **Gold leaf** — ultra-thin hammered gold, slightly irregular with cracks and overlap seams. Warm, rich, classical luxury.
- **Silver leaf** — cooler tone, tarnishes darker over time.
- **Metallic foil (hot stamp)** — machine-applied, more uniform. Modern luxury, packaging, editorial.
- **Copper leaf** — warm, shifts to verdigris patina. Craft, steampunk, industrial warmth.

Gold leaf has organic irregularity; foil is uniform. Both are reflective and luxurious.

**Pairs well with**: Dark backgrounds (black + gold is timeless), jewel tones, typography/lettering, luxury branding, Art Deco.

**Conflicts with**: Minimalism (unless very sparse), matte/raw aesthetics, casual/budget looks.
