# Film Stocks & Analog

Film stocks are not interchangeable — each has a distinct color science, grain structure, contrast curve, and emotional register. Referencing a film stock by name in a prompt invokes its entire visual character. Analog artifacts (grain, flare, halation) add texture and imperfection that signals "real" even in generated images.

---

## Color Negative Film

Color negative film produces a negative image that is printed or scanned. The dominant consumer and professional format. C-41 processing (standard, available everywhere). Characterized by wide exposure latitude — forgiving of over- and underexposure.

### Kodak Portra 160

**What it is**: Professional color negative, ISO 160. The finest-grain Portra. Daylight balanced. Introduced 1998, updated 2010.

**Visual effect**: Extremely fine grain, almost invisible in normal prints. Balanced, natural color saturation — not punchy, not muted. Skin tones are the priority: warm, accurate, dimensional. Highlight rolloff is gentle — overexposed areas fade gracefully rather than clipping hard. The overall look is polished, clean, and natural.

**Pairs well with**:
- Studio/controlled light — the low ISO needs plenty of light
- Medium format — fine grain + large negative = almost grainless, creamy images
- Beauty and fashion — skin tone accuracy is the core strength
- Soft directional light — the gentle tonality shines in subtle gradations
- 85mm portrait lens — the classic Portra portrait setup

**Conflicts with**:
- Low light / available light — ISO 160 needs bright conditions or flash
- Gritty/raw aesthetic — too clean and polished
- High contrast drama — Portra is gentle, not dramatic

**Example combination**: Portra 160 + Hasselblad + soft window light + 80mm f/2.8 → creamy, virtually grainless portrait, warm natural skin tones, gentle highlight rolloff, medium format richness

---

### Kodak Portra 400

**What it is**: Professional color negative, ISO 400. The most popular Portra — versatile enough for studio and available light. The default "film look" in modern photography.

**Visual effect**: Very fine grain, visible at large prints but never intrusive. Same skin tone priority as 160 but with slightly more grain texture. Warm midtones, muted but not desaturated color palette. The highlight rolloff is famously beautiful — overexposed Portra 400 has a warm, golden, dreamy quality that's become the defining "film aesthetic" on social media. Slight pastel quality in the shadows.

**Pairs well with**:
- Overexposure (+1 to +2 stops) — the highlight rolloff is the signature move, overexposed Portra is a genre
- Golden hour — the warm cast amplifies Portra's warmth
- Wedding/lifestyle photography — the romantic, warm quality defines the genre
- 35mm — the grain adds character without being heavy
- Available light — ISO 400 handles most daylight conditions

**Conflicts with**:
- Cool/clinical color palettes — Portra is inherently warm
- Punchy/saturated color — Portra is muted by design
- High-contrast graphic work — the gentle tonal curve softens everything

**Example combination**: Portra 400 + overexposed +1 stop + golden hour backlight + 35mm → warm, dreamy, grain visible but gentle, blown highlights with golden glow, the definitive "film look"

**Prompt cues**: warm natural color film photograph; soft pastel highlights; fine film grain; true-to-life skin tones

---

### Kodak Portra 800

**What it is**: Professional color negative, ISO 800. The fast Portra — designed for low light while maintaining the Portra color science.

**Visual effect**: More visible grain than 160/400 but still controlled. Same warm skin tone priority. Slightly more contrast than slower Portras. The grain adds texture and grit while maintaining the characteristic warmth. Works in conditions where 160 and 400 can't.

**Pairs well with**:
- Low light / indoor available light — the core use case
- Evening/dusk shooting — enough speed for fading light
- Concert/event photography — fast enough for handheld in dim venues
- Grain as texture — the visible grain is a feature at this speed

**Conflicts with**:
- Studio/controlled light — overkill, use 160 or 400 for cleaner results
- Ultra-clean rendering — grain is visible and present
- Large format — Portra 800 is mainly a 35mm and 120 stock

---

### Kodak Ektar 100

**What it is**: Consumer/prosumer color negative, ISO 100. The world's finest-grain color negative film. Daylight balanced.

**Visual effect**: Ultra-fine grain, extremely high saturation and color punch. Much more vivid than Portra — reds are fiery, blues are deep, greens are electric. High contrast with strong tonal separation. Skin tones lean slightly red/warm and can look oversaturated compared to Portra. This is a landscape and product film, not a portrait film.

**Pairs well with**:
- Landscape — saturated color is the point, and the fine grain holds detail
- Product/still life — vivid, punchy color representation
- Bright daylight — ISO 100 needs full sun
- Graphic/bold compositions — the high saturation supports bold visual statements

**Conflicts with**:
- Flattering skin tones — Ektar oversaturates skin, especially reds
- Muted/pastel color — Ektar is the opposite of muted
- Low light — ISO 100, needs bright conditions

**Example combination**: Ektar 100 + midday sun + landscape + wide angle + f/11 → punchy saturated greens and blues, deep shadows, vivid sky, ultra-fine grain, maximum color impact

---

### Kodak Gold 200

**What it is**: Consumer color negative, ISO 200. The ubiquitous "yellow box" film. Cheap, accessible, the film most people grew up with.

**Visual effect**: Warm, slightly amber/golden cast. Moderate grain — visible but not heavy. Colors lean warm and slightly muted compared to professional stocks. A nostalgic, familiar quality — this is what family photos, vacation snapshots, and disposable camera pictures look like. The warmth is less refined than Portra: more golden/amber than creamy.

**Pairs well with**:
- Nostalgia/memory — the film of childhood photos
- Warm daylight — amplifies the golden cast
- Snapshot/candid aesthetic — the consumer quality is the point
- Point-and-shoot cameras — the intended pairing

**Conflicts with**:
- Professional precision — Gold is a consumer stock with looser tolerances
- Cool/clinical tones — too warm and golden
- Fine detail — the grain is present and consumer-grade

---

### Fuji Superia 400

**What it is**: Consumer color negative, ISO 400. Fuji's answer to Kodak Gold but with different color science — cooler, greener.

**Visual effect**: Cooler tones than Kodak stocks — slight green/cyan cast in shadows, less warm overall. Moderate grain. Greens are vivid, skin tones lean slightly cool/neutral. The Fuji color palette is distinct from Kodak: where Kodak is warm and amber, Fuji is cool and green.

**Pairs well with**:
- Green environments — foliage looks lush on Fuji stocks
- Cooler/neutral mood — less warmth than Kodak
- Urban/street — the cool cast suits concrete and mixed light
- Overcast conditions — the cool film + cool light creates a unified tone

**Conflicts with**:
- Warm/golden mood — Fuji is cooler by nature
- Warm skin tones — the green cast can make skin look pallid
- Sunset/golden hour warmth — fights the film's cool lean

---

### Fuji Pro 400H (Discontinued)

**What it is**: Professional color negative, ISO 400. Discontinued 2021. Legendary in wedding/portrait work. The most mourned discontinued film stock of the digital era.

**Visual effect**: Soft, pastel color rendering. Skin tones lean slightly cool/pink rather than warm (opposite of Portra's warmth). Greens shift toward mint/sage. Highlights are creamy and smooth. Low contrast. Almost "watercolor" quality. Fine grain for 400 speed.

**Pairs well with**: Soft natural light, overcast conditions, pastel environments, bridal/editorial portraiture, garden settings, cool-toned subjects.

**Conflicts with**: High contrast, punchy saturated color, harsh flash, gritty urban.

---

### Agfa Vista / Agfa Color

**What it is**: Budget-to-mid-range European consumer color films. Agfa Vista 200/400 were staples with a distinctive color palette.

**Visual effect**: Slightly muted, earthy. Less saturated than Kodak consumer films. Greens lean olive/muted. Reds go slightly brick. A distinctly "European" color quality. Warmer overall but without Kodak's golden warmth.

**Pairs well with**: European street photography, documentary, autumn/earth-toned scenes, muted vintage aesthetics.

**Conflicts with**: Vivid tropical color, clean modern looks, high-saturation subjects.

---

### Kodak Vision3 250D / 500T (Cinema Stocks)

**What it is**: Professional motion picture film. 250D = daylight-balanced, clean natural color. 500T = tungsten-balanced, the most widely used cinema stock in the world. The film behind most modern films shot on celluloid.

**Visual effect**: 250D — clean, slightly warm, moderate grain, excellent highlight rolloff. The "this looks like a proper movie" baseline. 500T — slightly more grain, beautiful under artificial/mixed light, the specific cinema magic. Warm under tungsten; cool/blue in unfiltered daylight.

**Pairs well with**: Cinematic compositions, narrative scenes, professional lighting, any genre. "Shot on 500T" is powerful shorthand for cinematic quality.

**Conflicts with**: Lo-fi (too polished), vintage/retro (modern stocks), heavily stylized grades (the natural rendering fights extreme manipulation).

## Slide / Reversal Film

Slide film (transparency, reversal) produces a positive image directly on the film — what you see is the final image. Much less exposure latitude than negative film (must be exposed precisely). E-6 processing. Higher contrast and saturation than negative. The projected slide is the original — no printing generation loss.

### Kodachrome

**What it is**: Kodak's legendary slide film. K-14 processing (unique, complex). Discontinued 2009, last roll processed December 30, 2010. Available in 25 and 64 ISO versions.

**Visual effect**: Rich, saturated but not garish color. Photographer Steve McCurry described it: "a great color palette... not too garish... more poetry in it, a softness, an elegance." Warm reds and yellows, deep but not electric blues. The colors feel real but heightened — a curated version of reality. Fine grain. The defining look of National Geographic magazine and mid-20th century color photography.

**Pairs well with**:
- Documentary/travel — the National Geographic aesthetic
- Warm daylight — Kodachrome's reds and yellows glow in sun
- 35mm — the classic Kodachrome format
- Nostalgic mid-century color — this IS that look
- Medium contrast scenes — Kodachrome handles them beautifully

**Conflicts with**:
- Available shooting — the film hasn't been manufactured since 2009
- High contrast scenes — slide film's narrow latitude clips easily
- Cool/desaturated palettes — Kodachrome is warm and vivid

**Example combination**: Kodachrome 64 + 35mm + travel/street scene + warm daylight → rich warm color with poetic saturation, fine grain, National Geographic quality, mid-century documentary warmth

---

### Fuji Velvia 50

**What it is**: Professional slide film, ISO 50. The landscape photographer's film. E-6 processing. Still manufactured.

**Visual effect**: Extremely high color saturation — the most vivid of any slide film. Greens are electric, blues are deep and intense, reds are fiery. Very high contrast with strong tonal separation. Extremely fine grain with the highest resolving power of any slide film (160 lines/mm). The colors are heightened beyond natural but in a way that feels "more real than real." Where Kodachrome has poetry, Velvia has intensity.

**Pairs well with**:
- Landscape — the defining landscape film
- Bold saturated scenes — blue sky, green foliage, autumn colors
- Golden hour and blue hour — the saturation amplifies the color drama
- Large format — Velvia + 4x5 = maximum detail and color intensity
- Deep depth of field — landscape compositions front to back

**Conflicts with**:
- Skin tones — Velvia oversaturates skin to an unnatural degree
- Subtle/muted palettes — Velvia makes everything vivid
- Low light — ISO 50 needs full sun or a tripod
- Long exposures — Velvia suffers from reciprocity failure (color shift on long exposures)

**Example combination**: Velvia 50 + 4x5 large format + landscape + golden hour → explosively saturated sky and foliage, no visible grain, extreme sharpness front to back, the definitive landscape film look

---

### Fuji Provia 100F

**What it is**: Professional slide film, ISO 100. E-6 processing. Fuji's "neutral" slide film — less saturated than Velvia, more versatile.

**Visual effect**: Accurate, natural color with moderate saturation — vivid but not hyper-real like Velvia. Extremely fine grain. Neutral color balance — neither warm (Kodachrome) nor ultra-vivid (Velvia). The "honest" slide film that renders things as they are. Good for subjects where accuracy matters more than impact.

**Pairs well with**:
- Product/commercial — accurate color representation
- Architecture — neutral rendering, fine detail
- Any subject where color accuracy matters — Provia doesn't editorialize
- Studio/controlled conditions — the neutral balance responds well to precise lighting

**Conflicts with**:
- Maximum impact/saturation — Velvia does that job
- Warm nostalgic mood — Provia is neutral, not warm
- Gritty/textured aesthetic — too clean and precise

## Black and White Film

### Kodak Tri-X 400

**What it is**: The most iconic black and white film. ISO 400. Gelatin-silver process. Introduced ~1940. The film of photojournalism, street photography, and documentary. Still manufactured.

**Visual effect**: Pronounced, characterful grain — not fine, not coarse, distinctly "Tri-X." Rich mid-tones with strong blacks. High but not extreme contrast. Push-processable to 800, 1600, even 3200 with increasing grain and contrast. The grain has a chunky, organic quality that's immediately recognizable. This is what "black and white photography" looks like in the collective imagination.

**Pairs well with**:
- Street photography — the combination of Tri-X + 35mm + 28-50mm lens IS street photography
- Push processing — shooting at 800 or 1600 in low light, developing at a higher time, increases grain and contrast for a raw, urgent look
- High contrast scenes — Tri-X handles the tonal extremes
- Photojournalism/documentary — the film's heritage IS this genre
- Hard/directional light — the contrast translates beautifully to B&W

**Conflicts with**:
- Fine grain/smooth tonal rendering — Tri-X is characterful, not smooth
- Color (obviously) — but worth noting the "Tri-X look" implies monochrome
- Delicate/ethereal mood — Tri-X is gritty and present

**Example combination**: Tri-X 400 pushed to 800 + 28mm + street scene + hard light → chunky grain, deep blacks, bright highlights, photojournalistic urgency, classic documentary energy

**Prompt cues**: black and white film photograph; pronounced gritty film grain; deep blacks and rich midtones; high contrast

---

### Ilford HP5 Plus 400

**What it is**: ISO 400 black and white film. Tri-X's main competitor. Gelatin-silver process. Push-processable to 3200. Introduced 1989.

**Visual effect**: Slightly smoother grain than Tri-X. Lower base contrast — more gradual tonal transitions. The shadows are more open (more detail in dark areas). Where Tri-X is punchy and contrasty, HP5 is smoother and more forgiving. Both have strong grain, but HP5's feels more even and less chunky.

**Pairs well with**:
- Portraiture in B&W — the smoother tonality flatters more than Tri-X
- Soft/overcast light — HP5's lower contrast suits gentle conditions
- Push processing — handles it well with less contrast spike than Tri-X
- General-purpose B&W — more versatile than the more opinionated Tri-X

**Conflicts with**:
- Maximum contrast/punch — Tri-X does that better
- Ultra-fine grain — HP5 is still grainy at ISO 400
- The "classic photojournalism" look — that's Tri-X territory culturally

---

### Ilford Delta 3200

**What it is**: Ultra-high-speed black and white film. True ISO ~1000-1250, designed to be push-processed to 3200 or 6400. Tabular grain.

**Visual effect**: Heavy, prominent grain with a distinctive character. High contrast. The grain becomes a dominant visual element — the image is as much about texture as content. Deep blacks, chalky highlights. The look is raw, nocturnal, and urgent. This is the film for shooting in near-darkness.

**Pairs well with**:
- Extreme low light — concerts, clubs, nighttime street
- Grain as primary aesthetic — the texture IS the image
- High contrast B&W — deep blacks against bright highlights
- Raw/punk energy — the heavy grain reads as unpolished and urgent
- 35mm — the grain is already heavy; smaller format amplifies it

**Conflicts with**:
- Fine detail — the grain obscures it
- Clean rendering — the opposite of this film's character
- Controlled/studio work — overkill, use a slower film

**Example combination**: Delta 3200 + nighttime street + available light + 35mm → heavy grain, faces emerging from darkness, high contrast, raw nocturnal energy

---

### Ilford FP4 Plus 125

**What it is**: ISO 125 fine-grain black and white film. The "careful" B&W film — where HP5 and Tri-X are fast and gritty, FP4 is slow and precise.

**Visual effect**: Very fine grain for a B&W film. Smooth tonal gradations — long scale from deep black to bright white with many shades between. Low contrast curve. The image is detailed, resolved, and deliberate. Reads as contemplative and precise.

**Pairs well with**:
- Landscape in B&W — the fine grain holds detail, the tonal range captures gradations
- Medium and large format — fine grain + big negative = virtually grainless
- Studio/controlled light — the low ISO needs plenty of light
- Architectural photography — detail and precision
- Long exposure — FP4 handles reciprocity well

**Conflicts with**:
- Low light — ISO 125 needs bright conditions
- Gritty/raw aesthetic — too refined
- Action/movement — slow film + needed exposure = motion blur risk

---

### Kodak T-Max 100/400

**What it is**: Modern tabular-grain (T-grain) B&W films. Sharper and finer-grained than traditional Tri-X/HP5. The "clinical" B&W option.

**Visual effect**: T-Max 100 — extremely fine grain, sharp, slightly cooler/more neutral tone. T-Max 400 — noticeably finer grain than Tri-X at the same speed. More "modern" and less "characterful." Sharper but with less romantic grain texture.

**Pairs well with**: Fashion B&W, architectural photography, clean modern monochrome, technical work.

**Conflicts with**: Vintage/nostalgic (too modern-feeling), gritty photojournalism (too clean), expressionist grain (too refined).

---

### Ilford Pan F Plus 50

**What it is**: Ultra-fine grain, ultra-slow (ISO 50) B&W film. The sharpest, most detailed traditional B&W available.

**Visual effect**: Almost grainless at normal print sizes. Extremely fine detail. Smooth, long tonal range. Can feel almost too clean — lacks the "character" grain of faster films. The large-format look on 35mm.

**Pairs well with**: Studio portraiture, still life, architecture, any subject where detail and smoothness matter.

**Conflicts with**: Gritty/street aesthetics, heavy grain, low light (needs lots of light), lo-fi.

---

### Fomapan 100/200/400

**What it is**: Czech-made B&W film by Foma Bohemia. Budget-priced with distinctive character. Pronounced, chunky grain especially at 400.

**Visual effect**: Heavy, clumpy grain (especially 400). Lower resolving power than Ilford/Kodak. Compressed tonal range. The grain has a specific "clumpy" quality different from Tri-X or HP5. Feels older, more Eastern European.

**Pairs well with**: Lo-fi photography, Eastern European aesthetic, street photography, moody/atmospheric work, punk/DIY.

**Conflicts with**: Clean precision, fine art smoothness, commercial polish.

## Specialty / Distinctive Film

### CineStill 800T

**What it is**: Kodak Vision3 500T cinema film with the remjet anti-halation layer removed, repackaged for C-41 still photography processing. Tungsten balanced (3200K). ISO 800. Introduced 2012.

**Visual effect**: The signature look is **halation** — a red/orange glow around bright highlights (neon signs, street lights, candles) caused by the removed anti-halation layer. Light that would normally be absorbed scatters back through the red-sensitive layer, creating ethereal halos. Tungsten balance means daylight shots go blue unless corrected. Under tungsten/artificial light, colors are neutral and cinematic. High speed for low-light shooting. The overall look is nocturnal, cinematic, and dreamy.

**Pairs well with**:
- Nighttime urban scenes — neon signs with halation halos are the iconic CineStill shot
- Artificial/tungsten light — the film is balanced for it, colors render naturally
- Cinematic mood — it IS cinema film, the motion picture color science carries over
- Available light — ISO 800 handles dim conditions
- Wet streets — reflections of halated lights multiply the effect

**Conflicts with**:
- Daylight without correction — the image goes strongly blue (unless you want that)
- Clean/precise rendering — halation is inherently soft and glowing
- Bright highlights near edges — halation can be extreme and distracting
- Natural/documentary — the halation is an obvious stylistic signature

**Example combination**: CineStill 800T + nighttime city + neon signs + wet streets → halation halos around every light source, warm neon against cool shadows, reflections in puddles glowing red, cinematic dream

---

### Lomography Films (Various)

**What it is**: A family of experimental and novelty films from the Lomography brand — including high-saturation color stocks, redscale (film loaded backwards for red cast), purple-tinted stocks, and extreme-speed options.

**Visual effect**: Varies by stock, but the common thread is intentional imperfection and experimentation. Colors are often cross-processed, shifted, or oversaturated. Grain varies. The brand philosophy is "don't think, just shoot" — the aesthetic is deliberately lo-fi, experimental, and accident-embracing.

**Pairs well with**:
- Experimental/art photography — the unpredictability is the point
- Lo-fi aesthetic — plastic cameras, light leaks, soft focus
- Bright/saturated subjects — the film amplifies color weirdness
- Cross-processing — many Lomo stocks are designed for it

**Conflicts with**:
- Predictable, controlled results — Lomo is unpredictable by design
- Professional/commercial — too inconsistent
- Natural color — Lomo stocks shift color deliberately

## Analog Artifacts

Physical characteristics of film photography that arise from the medium itself — imperfections that have become aesthetically valued.

### Film Grain

**What it is**: Visible particles of metallic silver (B&W) or dye clouds (color) in the developed image. Caused by the random distribution of silver halide crystals in the emulsion. Larger crystals = more light sensitivity = more visible grain.

**Visual effect**: Adds organic texture and a sense of physical reality. Fine grain (low ISO) is smooth and barely visible. Coarse grain (high ISO, pushed processing) is chunky and dominant. Grain varies frame to frame — unlike digital noise, it's never perfectly uniform. It reads as "analog," "real," and "tactile."

**Pairs well with**:
- Any film stock reference — grain is inherent to film
- B&W photography — grain and monochrome are deeply linked
- Vintage/nostalgic mood — grain signals a physical, pre-digital medium
- Underexposure — shadow grain is more visible and textured
- Push processing — amplifies grain significantly

**Conflicts with**:
- Ultra-clean digital rendering — grain and digital sharpness fight
- Smooth skin/beauty — grain adds texture to everything including skin
- Vector/flat illustration styles — grain is photographic, not graphic

---

### Light Leaks

**What it is**: Unintended light entering the camera body through gaps in the housing, fogging portions of the film with streaks, washes, or patches of color (often amber, red, or magenta).

**Visual effect**: Warm color washes, typically at the edges or corners of the frame. Streaks of amber or red cutting across the image. Some frames may be heavily fogged, others barely touched. The effect is unpredictable and accidental — every light leak is unique.

**Pairs well with**:
- Disposable/toy camera aesthetic — light leaks are a hallmark of cheap cameras
- Lomography — the brand embraces light leaks
- Warm/nostalgic mood — the color washes add warmth
- Double exposure — both are "accidents" of the medium

**Conflicts with**:
- Controlled/precise imagery — light leaks are chaos
- Cool color palettes — the leaks are typically warm
- Professional/commercial — reads as accidental (unless deliberately stylized)

---

### Lens Flare

**What it is**: Light scattering inside the lens when a bright source (sun, lamp) hits the glass directly or from just outside the frame. Caused by internal reflections between lens elements. Multi-coated lenses reduce it; vintage/uncoated lenses amplify it.

**Visual effect**: Streaks, hexagonal or circular ghost shapes, overall haze/wash that reduces contrast, and warm color shifts. The shape of flare elements is determined by the aperture blade count (hexagonal from 6 blades, octagonal from 8). Anamorphic cinema lenses produce distinctive horizontal streak flares.

**Pairs well with**:
- Backlit golden hour — shooting toward the sun produces natural flare
- Vintage lenses — older coatings produce more flare
- Dreamy/romantic mood — the haze and warmth soften the image
- Anamorphic/cinematic — horizontal flare streaks signal cinema
- Warm tones — flare adds warmth and reduces contrast

**Conflicts with**:
- Sharp/contrasty rendering — flare reduces contrast and adds haze
- Cool/clinical tones — flare is warm
- Precise control — flare is somewhat unpredictable

**Example combination**: Lens flare + backlit golden hour + vintage lens + portrait → warm haze washing across the frame, circular ghosts, reduced contrast, dreamy romantic quality

---

### Halation

**What it is**: A glow or halo around bright highlights on film, caused by light passing through the emulsion, reflecting off the film base, and scattering back into the red-sensitive layer. The anti-halation backing on most film absorbs this; removing it (as CineStill does) creates pronounced halation.

**Visual effect**: Soft red/orange glow radiating outward from bright light sources — neon signs, candles, street lights, any specular highlight. The glow bleeds into surrounding areas, creating an ethereal, dreamy quality. Different from lens flare (which is in the lens); halation is in the film itself.

**Pairs well with**:
- CineStill 800T — the film most associated with halation
- Nighttime/artificial light — bright sources against dark areas maximize the effect
- Neon signs — the iconic halation subject
- Cinematic mood — halation reads as filmic and dreamlike
- Warm highlights — the red channel glow amplifies warmth

**Conflicts with**:
- Clean/precise highlights — halation is inherently soft and bloomy
- Daytime/even lighting — needs bright point sources against darkness
- Sharp detail in highlight areas — halation obscures it

---

### Vignetting

**What it is**: Darkening at the corners and edges of the frame relative to the center. Caused by light falloff at the lens periphery (natural), lens hood/filter obstruction (mechanical), or internal lens element shading (optical). Can be reduced by stopping down 2-3 stops.

**Visual effect**: A natural frame-within-a-frame that draws attention to the center. The darkened edges create a tunnel or spotlight effect. Subtle vignetting feels natural and barely noticed. Heavy vignetting feels vintage, lo-fi, or deliberately moody.

**Pairs well with**:
- Centered subjects — vignetting reinforces center focus
- Vintage/retro aesthetic — vignetting signals old lenses and old processes
- Portraits — the darkened edges frame the face
- Wide-open aperture — vignetting is strongest wide open, decreasing as you stop down

**Conflicts with**:
- Edge-to-edge sharpness and brightness — vignetting literally darkens the edges
- Panoramic/wide compositions where the edges matter
- Modern/clean aesthetic — vignetting reads as vintage

---

### Chromatic Aberration

**What it is**: Color fringing caused by a lens's failure to focus all wavelengths at the same point. Different colors of light refract at slightly different angles, creating colored outlines around high-contrast edges — typically purple/magenta or green.

**Visual effect**: Colored fringing, most visible at high-contrast boundaries (dark branches against bright sky, text against white). Lateral CA appears at frame edges; longitudinal CA appears as color shifts in out-of-focus areas. Subtle CA reads as vintage/optically imperfect. Heavy CA is distracting.

**Pairs well with**:
- Vintage lens aesthetic — old lenses have more CA
- Slight optical imperfection — a touch of CA adds analog character
- Wide-open aperture — CA is strongest at wide apertures
- Lo-fi/disposable camera look — cheap plastic lenses produce heavy CA

**Conflicts with**:
- Clean/precise rendering — CA is a flaw
- Modern lens quality — good modern lenses minimize CA
- Heavy amounts — a little adds character, a lot is distracting

---

### Motion Blur

**What it is**: Blurring caused by movement during exposure — either the subject moving or the camera moving (or both). Longer shutter speeds increase blur.

**Visual effect**: Streaks and smears in the direction of movement. A moving subject becomes a ghost while static elements stay sharp (if camera is still). A moving camera blurs everything while a flash-frozen subject stays sharp (panning). Motion blur conveys speed, energy, and the passage of time.

**Pairs well with**:
- Long exposure — intentional creative blur
- Panning — subject sharp, background streaked
- Action/sports — conveying speed
- Nighttime — car light trails, crowd movement
- Direct flash + slow shutter — sharp flash-frozen subject with ghosted ambient blur behind it

**Conflicts with**:
- Sharp, frozen detail — motion blur is the opposite
- Static/still subjects — no movement to blur
- Short shutter speeds — insufficient time for blur to register

---

### Double Exposure

**What it is**: Two separate images exposed on the same frame of film, overlapping and blending together. Can be done intentionally in-camera or by rewinding and re-exposing the roll.

**Visual effect**: Ghostly overlap of two scenes. Bright areas of one image dominate; dark areas allow the other image to show through. Creates surreal, dreamlike composites. The two images blend unpredictably — some areas show one scene, some show the other, some blend both.

**Pairs well with**:
- Surreal/dreamlike imagery — the overlap is inherently surreal
- Portraits + nature — face overlaid with trees, flowers, landscapes (a classic combo)
- Experimental/art photography — the unpredictability is the point
- Silhouettes — dark subject shape fills with the second exposure

**Conflicts with**:
- Clarity/readability — two overlapping images compete for attention
- Clean composition — double exposure is inherently busy
- Precise control — the blend is partially unpredictable

---

### Cross Processing

**What it is**: Deliberately developing film in the wrong chemistry — typically slide film (E-6) in C-41 negative chemistry, or negative film in E-6 reversal chemistry. The chemical mismatch produces shifted, unnatural colors.

**Visual effect**: E-6 in C-41 (the common version): boosted contrast, shifted hues, increased saturation with unpredictable color casts — greens go yellow, blues go cyan, skin tones shift toward magenta or yellow. The specific shift depends on the film stock. The look is punchy, contrasty, and deliberately wrong. Peaked in 1990s fashion photography (Nick Knight, Anton Corbijn).

**Pairs well with**:
- Fashion editorial — the shifted colors are deliberately stylized
- 1990s aesthetic — cross processing defines 90s fashion photography
- Experimental/art — the unpredictability is creative
- High saturation scenes — the processing amplifies vivid color

**Conflicts with**:
- Natural/accurate color — cross processing is the opposite
- Predictable results — each stock + chemistry combo produces different shifts
- Subtle/muted palettes — cross processing is loud

---

### Expired Film Look

**What it is**: The specific color shifts and degradation from film aging past its expiration date. Different stocks degrade differently depending on storage.

**Visual effect**: Color shifts (often toward magenta, green, or amber). Increased grain. Reduced contrast. Base fog (milky shadows). Unpredictable color casts. Reduced sensitivity. The older and worse the storage, the more extreme the effects.

**Pairs well with**: Nostalgic/vintage aesthetics, dreamlike imagery, lo-fi, Lomography, psychedelic/surreal subjects.

**Conflicts with**: Color accuracy, commercial work, clean modern aesthetics, predictable results.
