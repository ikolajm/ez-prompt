# Cameras & Lenses

The camera and lens determine the fundamental optical character of an image — perspective, depth, distortion, and format. In prompt composition, referencing a specific camera or lens type invokes the visual qualities associated with that gear, not just the subject matter.

---

## Film Formats

The size of the film (or sensor) changes the fundamental character of the image: resolution, grain structure, depth of field behavior, and aspect ratio.

### 35mm (Full Frame)

**What it is**: The standard photographic film format — 24x36mm frame on 135 film. Introduced by Leica in the 1920s, it became the dominant format for the 20th century. "Full frame" digital sensors match this size.

**Visual effect**: The baseline. Moderate depth of field, natural perspective, visible but controlled grain. The default visual language of photojournalism, street photography, and documentary work. When people picture "a photograph," they're usually picturing 35mm characteristics.

**Aspect ratio**: 3:2

**Associated cameras**: Leica M series, Canon AE-1, Nikon FM2, Pentax K1000 (film). Canon EOS R5, Sony A7 series, Nikon Z series (digital).

**Pairs well with**:
- Visible film grain — reinforces the analog, authentic quality
- 28-50mm focal lengths — the natural street/documentary range for this format
- Slightly underexposed or pushed processing — gritty, high-contrast photojournalism look

**Conflicts with**:
- Ultra-clean, noise-free rendering — fights the grain/texture expectation
- Medium format tonal smoothness — 35mm is grittier by nature

**Example combination**: 35mm film + 28mm wide angle + Kodak Tri-X 400 + available light → high-contrast black and white street photography, visible grain, deep shadows, documentary realism

---

### Medium Format

**What it is**: Film format using 120 roll film, producing frames between 6x4.5cm and 6x17cm — two to six times larger than 35mm. The larger negative captures more detail with finer grain and smoother tonal gradations.

**Visual effect**: Noticeably different from 35mm. Shallower depth of field at equivalent framing (larger format requires longer focal lengths). Smoother tonal transitions. Finer grain. An overall sense of richness and dimensionality. Skin tones look more dimensional. Highlights roll off more gently.

**Common aspect ratios**: 1:1 (6x6), 4:3 (6x4.5), 7:6 (6x7)

**Associated cameras**: Hasselblad 500C/500CM (6x6 square, NASA moon missions, fashion standard), Mamiya RZ67 (6x7, portrait workhorse), Rolleiflex (6x6 TLR, mid-century street), Fujifilm GFX series (modern digital medium format).

**Pairs well with**:
- Portrait and beauty work — the tonal smoothness flatters skin
- Fashion editorial — the square 6x6 crop is a strong compositional statement
- Soft directional lighting — medium format's tonal range shines with nuanced light
- Shallow depth of field — the format's natural tendency, lean into it

**Conflicts with**:
- Gritty, lo-fi aesthetics — medium format reads as polished and intentional
- Fast action/candid — the cameras are slow and deliberate by nature
- Heavy grain — counteracts the format's smoothness advantage

**Example combination**: Hasselblad 500C + 80mm f/2.8 + Kodak Portra 160 + soft window light → rich, warm portrait with square crop, smooth skin tones, gentle highlight rolloff, fine grain, fashion editorial quality

---

### Large Format

**What it is**: Sheet film 4x5 inches or larger (also 5x7, 8x10). Each exposure is a single sheet loaded in a dark holder. A 4x5 frame has 15 times the area of 35mm.

**Visual effect**: Extraordinary resolution and detail. Virtually grainless images with smooth, continuous tonal gradation. View camera tilt/shift movements allow precise focus plane control via the Scheimpflug principle — razor-thin focus at an angle, or everything sharp front to back.

**Associated cameras**: Speed Graphic, Sinar, Toyo, Linhof, Intrepid.

**Pairs well with**:
- Landscape — Ansel Adams's Zone System was built for this format
- Architectural photography — shift movements correct converging verticals
- Fine art portraiture — the detail and tonal range are unmatched
- Long exposures — the deliberate workflow encourages slow, considered work

**Conflicts with**:
- Spontaneity, candid, street — the camera is slow and conspicuous
- Action/movement — sheet film, tripod required, no motor drive
- Lo-fi or casual aesthetics — everything about large format reads as deliberate and premium

**Example combination**: 4x5 large format + 210mm lens + Fuji Velvia 50 + golden hour side light → hyper-detailed landscape with saturated but natural color, no visible grain, extreme depth of field via tilt, luminous quality

---

### Instant / Polaroid

**What it is**: Self-developing film that produces a print within minutes. Polaroid SX-70, Instax, Impossible Project.

**Visual effect**: Soft focus, muted and slightly shifted colors, a characteristic warm cast, and the iconic white border. Colors are less saturated and lean warm. Highlights can blow out with a milky quality. Nostalgic, imperfect, one-of-a-kind feeling.

**Pairs well with**:
- Warm, soft lighting — golden hour, window light amplify the warmth
- Casual/intimate subjects — the format reads as personal and unguarded
- Visible white border — strong compositional signifier, immediately recognizable
- Slightly overexposed — leans into the milky highlight quality

**Conflicts with**:
- Technical precision — the format is inherently soft and unpredictable
- High saturation — Polaroid colors are muted by nature
- Dark/moody tones — the warm cast and low contrast fight heavy shadows

**Example combination**: Polaroid SX-70 + soft afternoon light + slightly overexposed + white border → intimate, warm, nostalgic snapshot with muted pastels and dreamy softness

---

### Disposable Camera

**What it is**: Single-use cameras with fixed-focus plastic lenses and built-in flash. Kodak FunSaver, Fujifilm QuickSnap.

**Visual effect**: Soft/slightly blurry focus (plastic lens), harsh direct flash with hard shadows, oversaturated colors from cheap film, vignetting at edges. Amateur snapshot quality — imperfect, casual, spontaneous.

**Pairs well with**:
- Harsh on-camera flash — the defining look, don't soften it
- Nighttime/party scenes — flash overpowering darkness is the iconic disposable shot
- Oversaturated color — cheap film pushes reds and greens
- Slight motion blur — the slow shutter speed + flash freeze creates ghosting

**Conflicts with**:
- Sharp detail — the plastic lens physically cannot resolve it
- Studio-quality lighting — defeats the raw, amateur aesthetic
- Muted/desaturated tones — disposable film is punchy and oversaturated

**Example combination**: Disposable camera + direct flash + nighttime party + motion blur → blown-out faces against dark background, hard shadows, oversaturated skin tones, 90s nostalgia energy

---

### Smartphone / Computational

**What it is**: Small-sensor cameras (iPhone, Pixel, etc.) with wide-angle equivalent lenses (~26mm) and heavy computational processing — HDR stacking, noise reduction, portrait mode depth simulation.

**Visual effect**: Ultra-sharp but flat. Computational HDR compresses dynamic range, making shadows and highlights unnaturally even. Deep depth of field from the small sensor (everything in focus). Portrait mode simulates bokeh via software but the falloff looks synthetic compared to optical blur. Colors are saturated and consistent. Recognizably "phone photo."

**Pairs well with**:
- Social media/casual framing — selfie angles, arm's-length perspective
- Harsh or mixed lighting — computational HDR handles it (that's the point)
- Deep depth of field — lean into the "everything sharp" quality rather than fighting it

**Conflicts with**:
- Shallow depth of field — software bokeh always looks synthetic
- Film grain or analog texture — the computational pipeline removes imperfection
- Cinematic or editorial quality — the flat HDR look is the opposite of cinematic dynamic range

**Example combination**: iPhone + HDR + direct front flash + selfie angle → flat, sharp, social media snapshot with even exposure, deep focus, slight wide-angle distortion on close faces

## Focal Length

Focal length (measured in mm) controls field of view, perspective compression, and distortion. Shorter = wider view, more distortion. Longer = narrower view, more compression.

### Ultra Wide Angle (10-20mm)

**What it is**: Extremely wide field of view, approaching or exceeding 100 degrees.

**Visual effect**: Dramatic spatial exaggeration. Near objects appear much larger than far objects. Barrel distortion stretches edges. Straight lines near frame edges curve outward. Creates immersion and scale — rooms look bigger, landscapes more expansive, foreground subjects loom.

**Pairs well with**:
- Architectural interiors — exaggerates space, makes rooms look grand
- Low-angle shots — subject towers over viewer dramatically
- Leading lines — the distortion amplifies converging perspective
- Landscape with strong foreground — wildflowers in front, mountains behind, both huge

**Conflicts with**:
- Portraiture — extreme facial distortion at close range
- Flat/compressed backgrounds — ultra-wide does the opposite
- Subtle compositions — the lens imposes itself on every image

**Example combination**: 14mm ultra-wide + low angle + interior architecture + natural light from windows → dramatic room perspective, converging lines, exaggerated depth, light spilling across expansive space

---

### Wide Angle (24-35mm)

**What it is**: Wider than normal field of view (63-84 degrees). Workhorse for photojournalism, street photography, and environmental portraiture.

**Visual effect**: Expanded spatial relationships — foreground and background feel farther apart. Mild barrel distortion at edges. Deep depth of field comes easily. 28mm and 35mm are especially natural-feeling — wide enough for context, not so wide as to distort.

**Pairs well with**:
- Street photography — captures subject and environment together
- Environmental portraits — person within their world, context visible
- Documentary/photojournalism — the "being there" perspective
- Deep depth of field — everything sharp, situational awareness

**Conflicts with**:
- Tight headshot portraiture — mild but unflattering facial distortion under ~50mm
- Background isolation — wide angles keep backgrounds in focus and visible
- Compressed/stacked compositions — wide does the opposite of telephoto compression

**Example combination**: 35mm + f/8 + street scene + hard midday sun → deep focus, everything sharp foreground to background, expanded spatial relationships, documentary immediacy

**Prompt cues**: wide-angle shot; deep expanded space; near objects large, far objects small; stretched distortion at the frame edges

---

### Normal (40-60mm)

**What it is**: Approximates human vision — roughly 40-50 degree diagonal angle of view. The 50mm on 35mm format is the classic "nifty fifty."

**Visual effect**: Neutral perspective. No significant distortion or compression. Spatial relationships feel natural. The "invisible" focal length — it doesn't draw attention to itself.

**Pairs well with**:
- Naturalistic/documentary work — the optics disappear, the subject is the point
- Available light — 50mm primes are typically fast (f/1.4-f/1.8), great in low light
- Versatile framing — works for portraits, street, still life, most things

**Conflicts with**:
- Dramatic perspective effects — 50mm is deliberately neutral
- Strong compression or exaggeration — it does neither

**Example combination**: 50mm f/1.8 + available light + slightly underexposed + candid subject → naturalistic, unmanipulated feeling, moderate depth of field, honest rendering

---

### Short Telephoto / Portrait (70-105mm)

**What it is**: The classic portrait range. Mild perspective compression that flatters facial features.

**Visual effect**: Gentle compression — nose doesn't protrude, face looks proportional, background pushed farther away. At wide apertures (f/1.2-f/2.0), produces creamy background blur that isolates the subject. The 85mm f/1.4 is the iconic portrait lens — sharp eyes, smooth skin tones, melted background.

**Pairs well with**:
- Rembrandt or butterfly lighting — compression + directional shadow = classic editorial portrait
- Wide aperture (f/1.2-f/2.0) — maximizes the signature subject separation
- Medium format — both push toward rich tonality and shallow depth
- Beauty/fashion — flattering perspective, subject isolation, skin detail

**Conflicts with**:
- Environmental context — the tight framing and shallow depth erase surroundings
- Deep depth of field (f/11+) — defeats the lens's core strength
- Ultra-wide establishing shots — opposite intent

**Example combination**: 85mm f/1.4 + Rembrandt lighting + Kodak Portra 400 + wide open → warm, intimate portrait, one eye tack sharp, background dissolved into creamy warm bokeh, dimensional skin tones

**Prompt cues**: telephoto compression; shallow depth of field; background melted into soft blur

---

### Medium Telephoto (105-200mm)

**What it is**: Moderate compression, narrower field of view. Common for sports, events, and candid photography from a distance.

**Visual effect**: More pronounced background compression — objects at different distances appear closer together. Background elements magnified and softened. Subject clearly separated from environment.

**Pairs well with**:
- Candid/unposed subjects — shooting from distance preserves natural behavior
- Busy backgrounds — compression + shallow depth simplifies clutter
- Sports/action — close framing from a safe distance
- Layered urban scenes — compression stacks architectural elements

**Conflicts with**:
- Interiors/tight spaces — physically can't back up enough
- Environmental context — the narrow view isolates rather than contextualizes
- Wide-angle spatial storytelling

**Example combination**: 135mm f/2 + open shade + busy city background + wide open → subject sharp against a compressed wall of soft urban bokeh, background signs and lights become abstract color

---

### Telephoto (200-400mm)

**What it is**: Strong compression, very narrow field of view. The world appears stacked and flattened.

**Visual effect**: Extreme perspective compression — distant objects appear almost the same size as near objects. Backgrounds look like flat walls. Heat haze and atmospheric effects become visible. Depth of field is extremely shallow even at moderate apertures.

**Pairs well with**:
- Wildlife — close framing from safe distance
- Surveillance/voyeuristic aesthetic — the distance between photographer and subject is felt
- Stacked urban scenes — buildings, cars, people compressed into layers
- Atmospheric haze — long focal lengths pick up air density

**Conflicts with**:
- Wide establishing shots — the opposite intent
- Interiors — physically impossible
- Spatial depth/dimension — telephoto flattens everything

**Example combination**: 300mm + f/4 + heat haze + crowded street → flattened perspective, people stacked in layers, shimmering atmospheric distortion, voyeuristic distance

---

### Super Telephoto (400mm+)

**What it is**: Extreme magnification. Wildlife and astrophotography territory.

**Visual effect**: Maximum compression. Subjects at great distance appear close. Atmospheric distortion visible. Background reduced to abstract blur of color and shape.

**Pairs well with**:
- Wildlife/nature — the core use case
- Moon/astro — large celestial objects against landscapes
- Extreme subject isolation — nothing else in focus, ever

**Conflicts with**:
- Basically everything except distance subjects — the lens is single-purpose

**Example combination**: 600mm + f/4 + wildlife + early morning fog → animal portrait isolated against abstract wash of green/gold, paper-thin focus, atmospheric softness

## Historical & Alternative Processes

### Daguerreotype

**What it is**: The first commercially practical photographic process (1839). A silver-coated copper plate sensitized with iodine vapor, exposed in a camera, then developed over heated mercury. Each image is a unique, mirror-like object — no negative, no copies.

**Visual effect**: Mirror-like reflective silver surface — the image appears positive or negative depending on viewing angle. Extraordinary fine detail for the era. Monochromatic silver-gray tonal range. Visible plate edges and imperfections. Spectral, ghostly, precious quality. Exposures of 15-60+ seconds meant subjects had to hold perfectly still, creating a rigid formality.

**Pairs well with**:
- Formal portraiture — the rigid pose requirement became a compositional feature
- Dark backgrounds — common for the era, focuses attention on the subject
- Historical/period subjects — the process IS the 19th century
- Ornate frames and cases — daguerreotypes were presented as precious objects
- Memento mori aesthetic — the ghostly quality suits mortality themes

**Conflicts with**:
- Motion/action — impossible with long exposures
- Color — silver monochrome only
- Casual framing — the process demands formality
- Modern aesthetics — too antique

**Example combination**: Daguerreotype + formal portrait + dark background + silver-plate surface + visible plate edges → ghostly, precious, mirror-like tones, 1850s formal intensity

---

### Wet Plate Collodion

**What it is**: Photographic process (1851-1880s) where a glass or metal plate is coated with collodion, sensitized in silver nitrate, exposed while still wet, then developed immediately — all within about 15 minutes. The photographer is also a chemist.

**Visual effect**: Only sensitive to blue/UV light — blue eyes render pale/ghostly, red lips go dark, freckles become prominent. Visible pour lines from the collodion coating. Dark or uneven edges with chemical artifacts (drips, streaks). Hauntingly sharp in the center with organic falloff. A raw, handmade quality that's simultaneously precise and imperfect.

**Pairs well with**:
- Intense portraiture — the blue sensitivity creates eerie, compelling faces
- Civil War-era aesthetic — the process defines that visual period
- Dark/moody backgrounds — common studio practice
- Environmental portraits — subject in their world
- Memento mori — the ghostly rendering suits mortality themes

**Conflicts with**:
- Color accuracy — blue-sensitive only
- Movement — multi-second exposures
- Even edge-to-edge quality — chemical artifacts at edges
- Polished/digital aesthetics — too raw and handmade

**Example combination**: Wet plate collodion + intense portrait + blue-sensitive rendering + visible pour lines at edges + chemical artifacts → haunting, sharp center with organic edge decay, Civil War-era intensity

---

### Tintype

**What it is**: Variation of wet plate collodion on thin lacquered iron (not tin). Popular 1860s-1900s for affordable street and carnival portraiture. Sturdy and cheap — the "instant photo" of the 19th century.

**Visual effect**: Similar to wet plate but darker, more compressed tonal range (the dark iron base absorbs highlights). Lower contrast. Visible surface texture from the metal plate. Can appear laterally reversed (direct positive). Warm brownish or cool bluish tones depending on chemistry. Rougher, more democratic quality than daguerreotype.

**Pairs well with**:
- Americana / Old West aesthetic — the process defines frontier portraiture
- Folk/rough portraiture — less precious than daguerreotype
- Hand-tinted color accents — cheeks, jewelry, buttons painted by hand
- Carnival/sideshow imagery — the street portrait tradition

**Conflicts with**:
- High contrast / bright whites — the iron base suppresses them
- Precision/sharpness — rougher than daguerreotype
- Modern polish — too antique and rough

---

### Pinhole Camera

**What it is**: The simplest possible camera — a lightproof box with a tiny hole, no lens. Light passes through the pinhole and projects an inverted image. Exposure times range from seconds to hours. Can be made from anything.

**Visual effect**: Infinite depth of field (everything from inches to infinity is equally in/out of focus), soft overall image quality (no true sharpness anywhere), natural vignetting, motion blur from long exposures. No lens distortion whatsoever. Dreamy, primitive, lo-fi quality.

**Pairs well with**:
- Long exposure — inherent to the process
- Architecture / landscapes — the infinite depth of field suits sweeping scenes
- Experimental/art — the simplicity is the point
- Solargraph — multi-month exposures tracking the sun's path across the sky
- Contemplative/meditative mood — the slow process dictates the aesthetic

**Conflicts with**:
- Fast action / sharpness — physically impossible
- Shallow depth of field — infinite focus is the only option
- Handheld shooting — exposures are too long
- Crisp modern photography — everything is soft

**Example combination**: Pinhole camera + long exposure + architectural interior + infinite depth of field + natural vignetting → soft, ethereal, everything equally in focus, dreamy lo-fi

## Lens Types

### Prime Lens

**What it is**: Fixed focal length — no zoom. Simpler optical design allows wider maximum apertures and sharper images.

**Visual effect**: Generally sharper than zooms, especially wide open. More pleasing bokeh from simpler optical formula. The fixed focal length forces the photographer to move, producing more intentional compositions.

**Pairs well with**:
- Wide apertures — primes go wider than zooms (f/1.2-f/1.8 vs f/2.8-f/4)
- Low light — the wider aperture gathers more light
- Intentional, deliberate compositions — you can't zoom, you move

**Conflicts with**:
- Versatility/run-and-gun — one focal length, no flexibility
- Documentary situations where you can't control your distance

---

### Fisheye

**What it is**: Ultra-wide lens (8-16mm) with extreme barrel distortion — straight lines curve dramatically. Field of view up to 180 degrees. Circular fisheye produces a round image; full-frame fisheye fills the rectangle with distortion.

**Visual effect**: Spherical distortion of the entire frame. Center bulges toward viewer. Straight lines become curves. Surreal, immersive, or playful quality.

**Pairs well with**:
- Skateboarding/action sports — the iconic Thrasher magazine aesthetic
- Low-angle shots — looking up at subject with curved architecture wrapping around
- Psychedelic/surreal imagery — the distortion is the point
- Tight spaces — captures everything in a small room

**Conflicts with**:
- Natural proportions or straight lines
- Portraiture — extreme facial distortion
- Architectural accuracy — curves everything

**Example combination**: Fisheye + low angle + skatepark + harsh daylight → curved concrete forms, skater looming overhead, extreme spatial distortion, action sports energy

---

### Macro

**What it is**: Lens designed for extreme close-up photography, reproducing subjects at 1:1 ratio or greater. Common focal lengths: 60mm, 90mm, 100mm, 120mm.

**Visual effect**: Reveals detail invisible to the naked eye. Depth of field is razor-thin — at 1:1 and f/2.8, only a few millimeters in focus. The rest dissolves into blur. The world looks alien at this scale.

**Pairs well with**:
- Product detail shots — texture, material, craftsmanship visible
- Beauty/skin texture — pores, freckles, individual lashes
- Nature (insects, flowers) — the classic macro subject
- Abstract compositions — everyday objects become unrecognizable up close
- Ring flash or diffused light — even illumination on tiny subjects

**Conflicts with**:
- Context/environment — you can't see anything beyond the tiny focus area
- Deep depth of field — physically impossible at macro distances without extreme f-stops
- Movement — the thin focus plane means any shake loses the shot

**Example combination**: 100mm macro + f/4 + ring flash + water droplets on leaf → extreme detail, droplets sharp against soft green bokeh, light refracting through water, each vein of the leaf visible

---

### Tilt-Shift

**What it is**: Specialty lens that tilts the lens plane relative to the sensor (controlling focus plane angle) and shifts parallel to the sensor (controlling perspective).

**Visual effect**: Two distinct uses:
1. **Tilt for selective focus**: Creates a wedge-shaped focus plane — the "miniature/diorama" effect where only a thin band is sharp. Real-world scenes look like toy models.
2. **Shift for perspective control**: Corrects converging vertical lines when pointing up at buildings.

**Pairs well with**:
- Aerial/overhead city views — the miniature effect is strongest from above
- Architectural photography — shift corrects perspective distortion
- Saturated color — amplifies the toy-like quality of tilt miniatures
- Bright, even lighting — midday sun on a tilt-shift cityscape maximizes the model look

**Conflicts with**:
- Natural depth of field — tilt-shift focus is intentionally artificial
- Low light/moody scenes — the miniature effect needs clear visibility to read

**Example combination**: Tilt-shift + overhead cityscape + midday sun + saturated color → city looks like a detailed architectural model, thin band of focus across buildings, everything above and below softly blurred

---

### Anamorphic

**What it is**: Cinema lens with oval-shaped glass elements that squeeze a wide field of view onto a standard sensor, then "de-squeeze" in post or projection. Standard squeeze ratios: 1.33x and 2x. The defining lens of widescreen cinema — Blade Runner, any J.J. Abrams film, most blockbusters.

**Visual effect**: Distinctive horizontal lens flares (blue or warm streaks across the entire frame from point light sources). Oval/elliptical bokeh (not circular). Slight edge distortion and breathing (subtle zoom when focusing). Ultra-wide aspect ratios (2.39:1 or 2.76:1). The combination of these artifacts is so recognizable that "anamorphic" is shorthand for "cinematic."

**Pairs well with**:
- Neon / point light sources at night — each creates a horizontal flare streak
- Cinematic aspect ratios (2.39:1) — the native format
- Cyberpunk / sci-fi — anamorphic IS the Blade Runner look
- Shallow depth of field — oval bokeh is uniquely anamorphic
- Moody color grades — the lens artifacts enhance atmosphere
- Rain / wet surfaces — reflections create more flare sources

**Conflicts with**:
- Clinical sharpness — anamorphic has inherent softness and aberration
- Documentary realism — the flares and artifacts are obvious
- Square or 4:3 aspect ratios — anamorphic is ultra-wide
- Symmetrical/circular bokeh — anamorphic bokeh is oval

**Example combination**: Anamorphic lens + neon-lit rainy street + horizontal blue flares + oval bokeh + 2.39:1 aspect ratio → cinematic width, blue streaks across frame from every light source, unmistakably filmic

---

### Lensbaby / Selective Focus

**What it is**: Specialty lenses with a flexible or tilting optic that places a sharp "sweet spot" anywhere in the frame while the rest falls into heavy, swirly blur. A more accessible, creative cousin of tilt-shift.

**Visual effect**: One area of sharp focus surrounded by aggressive, swirly blur. More chaotic and dreamlike than tilt-shift's controlled band. The sweet spot can be moved by physically bending the lens. Creates a dreamy, fairy-tale quality.

**Pairs well with**:
- Portraits — single subject isolation with dreamy surround
- Flowers/macro — one bloom sharp, everything else dissolved
- Golden hour — warm light amplifies the dreamy quality
- Wedding/lifestyle — romantic, soft

**Conflicts with**:
- Landscape sharpness — most of the frame is blurred
- Architectural precision — too soft and uncontrolled
- Documentary — too stylized

---

## Optical Effects & Techniques

### Infrared Photography

**What it is**: Photography using film or sensors sensitive to near-infrared light (700-1200nm wavelength), invisible to the human eye. Achieved via infrared-sensitive film (Kodak Aerochrome, Ilford SFX) or a modified digital sensor with an IR-pass filter.

**Visual effect**: Green foliage turns bright white or magenta/pink (the "Wood effect" — chlorophyll reflects IR strongly). Skin becomes pale and waxy. Blue skies turn near-black. Veins become visible under skin. Atmospheric haze disappears. Color infrared (Aerochrome) produces surreal false-color with magentas and reds replacing greens.

**Pairs well with**:
- Landscape — the white/magenta foliage is the signature
- Surreal/dreamlike aesthetic — the color inversion is otherworldly
- Summer foliage — maximum chlorophyll = maximum IR effect
- High contrast black and white — IR B&W has deep blacks and bright whites
- Clear skies — IR turns blue sky nearly black

**Conflicts with**:
- Overcast/flat light — less dramatic IR effect
- Autumn/dead foliage — no chlorophyll to reflect IR
- Indoor scenes — limited IR sources
- Naturalistic color — IR is the opposite of natural

**Example combination**: Infrared + Kodak Aerochrome + forest landscape + summer + clear sky → magenta trees, deep black sky, surreal false-color, dreamlike and alien

---

### Solarization (Sabattier Effect)

**What it is**: Darkroom technique where a partially developed print is briefly re-exposed to light, causing partial tone reversal. Dark areas stay dark, but mid-tones reverse, creating distinctive bright outlines ("Mackie lines") at tonal boundaries. Made famous by Man Ray and Lee Miller.

**Visual effect**: Partial tone reversal, glowing bright outlines around subject edges, surreal metallic quality, flattened mid-tones. Otherworldly, psychedelic, alien quality.

**Pairs well with**:
- Portraiture — the edge glow on faces is striking
- Surrealism — Man Ray's territory
- High contrast — amplifies the reversal effect
- Metallic/silver tones — the reversal creates a chrome-like quality

**Conflicts with**:
- Naturalism — solarization is obviously manipulated
- Subtle tonal gradation — the reversal flattens mid-tones
- Color accuracy — the process distorts everything

---

### Prism / Crystal Photography

**What it is**: Holding a glass prism or crystal in front of the lens to bend, refract, and split light. A practical in-camera effect creating rainbow refractions and kaleidoscopic fragments.

**Visual effect**: Rainbow light refractions, duplicated/kaleidoscopic fragments of the scene, prismatic flares, warped reflections at frame edges. Adds an ethereal, otherworldly frame to the composition.

**Pairs well with**:
- Portraits — rainbow light on skin, fractured reflections framing the face
- Golden hour backlighting — light splits into spectral colors
- Fashion/creative — the effect is deliberately stylized
- Dreamy/romantic mood — the rainbows and fragments soften reality

**Conflicts with**:
- Clean compositions — the prism adds chaos
- Documentary/precision — too manipulated
- Controlled studio work — unpredictable effect

---

### Freelensing

**What it is**: Holding a detached lens in front of the camera body (not mounted), manually tilting by hand. Creates tilt-shift-like effects with unpredictable light leaks from the gap between lens and body. DIY, imprecise, one-of-a-kind.

**Visual effect**: Extremely shallow, tilted focal plane. Heavy warm light leaks (light entering the gap). Soft ethereal haze. Unpredictable focus. Every shot is unique. Handmade, romantic, imperfect quality.

**Pairs well with**:
- Golden hour backlighting — warm leaks amplified
- Portraits — dreamy, romantic isolation
- Wedding/lifestyle — the imperfection reads as intimate

**Conflicts with**:
- Repeatability — every shot is different
- Sharpness/precision — inherently soft and unpredictable
- Clean modern aesthetic — too chaotic

## Aperture

Aperture (f-stop) controls how much light enters the lens and directly determines depth of field. Lower f-numbers = wider opening = shallower focus.

### Wide Open (f/1.2 - f/2.0)

**Visual effect**: Extremely shallow depth of field. Only a thin slice of the scene is sharp. Background and foreground dissolve into smooth blur. Bright points of light become large, soft bokeh circles. Dreamy, intimate, isolated feeling.

**Pairs well with**:
- Portrait focal lengths (85-135mm) — maximum subject isolation
- Available/low light — the wide aperture gathers light
- Single subject with distant background — maximizes the blur differential
- Warm-toned light — bokeh circles pick up and amplify light color

**Conflicts with**:
- Group shots — too thin to keep multiple faces sharp
- Landscape — defeats the purpose of showing the scene
- Sharp background detail — physically impossible

**Example combination**: f/1.4 + 85mm + backlit golden hour + single subject → razor-thin focus on eyes, warm golden bokeh circles from backlighting, everything else dissolved

---

### Moderate (f/2.8 - f/5.6)

**Visual effect**: Subject sharp with noticeable but controlled background softening. Enough depth to keep a full face sharp. Most lenses are at their sharpest here. The workhorse range.

**Pairs well with**:
- Environmental portraiture — subject sharp, background present but soft
- Product photography — enough depth for the whole product
- Events/documentary — reliable sharpness with some separation

**Conflicts with**:
- Extreme bokeh/isolation — not wide enough
- Extreme sharpness front-to-back — not stopped down enough

---

### Stopped Down (f/8 - f/11)

**Visual effect**: Deep depth of field — most of the scene is sharp. Maximum lens sharpness (the "sweet spot"). Everything from foreground to background holds detail.

**Pairs well with**:
- Landscape — sharp front to back
- Architecture — detail throughout the frame
- Street photography — zone focus, everything in the scene readable

**Conflicts with**:
- Subject isolation — everything is sharp, nothing is separated
- Low light — small aperture needs more light or longer exposure

---

### Small Aperture (f/16 - f/22)

**Visual effect**: Nearly everything in focus. Star/sunburst effects on point light sources (diffraction through aperture blades). Slight overall softening from diffraction. Maximum depth of field.

**Pairs well with**:
- Sunstars — point the camera toward the sun or streetlights for defined ray patterns
- Landscape with extreme foreground-to-infinity sharpness
- Long exposures — the small aperture naturally extends exposure time

**Conflicts with**:
- Sharpness at the pixel level — diffraction softening is real at f/16+
- Bokeh — nonexistent at this aperture
- Handheld shooting — needs tripod or high ISO

**Example combination**: f/16 + wide angle + sunset + tripod → sunstar burst through trees, everything sharp from wildflowers at your feet to mountains on the horizon, long exposure smoothing any water

---

### Bokeh

**What it is**: The aesthetic quality of the out-of-focus areas in an image, from the Japanese word for "blur." Not the blur itself, but how the blur *looks*.

**Visual effect**: Good bokeh ("creamy" or "smooth") has soft, round highlight circles that blend gently. Harsh bokeh has hard-edged, bright-rimmed circles that look busy and distracting. Character depends on lens design — spherical aberration correction and aperture shape.

**Aperture blade count**: More blades (and curved blades) = rounder bokeh circles. Fewer blades = polygonal shapes (pentagons, hexagons). Some lenses are prized for unique bokeh: vintage Trioplan "soap bubble" bokeh, mirror/catadioptric "doughnut" bokeh.

**Pairs well with**:
- Wide apertures (f/1.2-f/2.0) — more blur = more visible bokeh character
- Backlit scenes — highlights behind subject become bokeh circles
- Point light sources (city lights, string lights, candles) — each becomes a distinct bokeh shape
- Portrait focal lengths — 85mm+ with wide aperture maximizes the effect

**Conflicts with**:
- Small apertures — no blur, no bokeh
- Even/flat backgrounds — no highlights to form bokeh circles
- Ultra-wide lenses — deep depth of field minimizes blur

**Example combination**: f/1.4 + 85mm + city lights at night behind subject → face sharp, background transforms into a field of large, soft, warm circles of light
