# Graphic Design Styles

Design movements and schools that define how visual information is organized, styled, and communicated. In prompt composition, referencing a graphic design style applies its entire system — grid, typography, color, and spatial philosophy — to the output.

---

### Swiss / International Typographic Style

**What it is**: 1950s-60s Swiss movement of mathematical grids, sans-serif typography, asymmetric layouts, and objective photography. The foundation of modern graphic design. Key designers: Josef Müller-Brockmann, Armin Hofmann, Emil Ruder. Key typefaces: Helvetica, Univers, Akzidenz-Grotesk.

**Visual effect**: Clean, ordered, rational. Rigid grid structure organizes all elements. Sans-serif type (usually Helvetica) at various sizes creates hierarchy. Flush-left, ragged-right text alignment. Large objective photographs. Minimal decoration — the grid and typography ARE the design. Reads as professional, universal, and authoritative.

**Pairs well with**:
- Sans-serif typography — Helvetica is the movement's typeface
- Grid-based layouts — mathematical, rigid, harmonious
- Objective/documentary photography — images as neutral information
- Black, white, and one accent color — the classic Swiss poster palette
- Asymmetric composition — dynamic but ordered
- Large-scale type — used as graphic element, not just text

**Conflicts with**:
- Decorative/ornamental elements — Swiss style rejects decoration
- Script/serif typography — sans-serif only
- Chaotic/expressive layouts — the grid prevents chaos
- Warm/emotional tone — Swiss is deliberately objective

**Example combination**: Swiss style + grid layout + Helvetica + black/white/red + large photograph → clean, authoritative poster, mathematical order, type as graphic element, objective and precise

### Bauhaus

**What it is**: 1919-1933 German school that unified art, craft, and industry. Geometric forms, primary colors, functional design, no unnecessary ornamentation. The marriage of art and mass production. Key figures: Gropius, Klee, Kandinsky, Moholy-Nagy, Breuer, Bayer.

**Visual effect**: Geometric, primary-colored, functional. Circle, square, triangle as fundamental forms. Red, yellow, blue as fundamental colors (with black and white). Clean sans-serif typography. Asymmetric but balanced compositions. Everything serves a purpose — no decoration for its own sake. Reads as modern, intellectual, and democratically accessible.

**Pairs well with**:
- Primary colors (red, yellow, blue) + black + white — the Bauhaus palette
- Basic geometric shapes — circle, square, triangle
- Sans-serif typography — Herbert Bayer's universal alphabet
- Flat/graphic rendering — no gradients, no texture
- Asymmetric balance — dynamic but harmonious
- Industrial materials — steel, glass, concrete

**Conflicts with**:
- Ornamental/decorative — Bauhaus rejects unnecessary decoration
- Organic/flowing forms — Bauhaus is geometric
- Complex color palettes — the primary palette is restrictive
- Historical/traditional aesthetics — Bauhaus is forward-looking

**Example combination**: Bauhaus + primary colors + geometric shapes + sans-serif type + white background → red circle, yellow triangle, blue square, clean type, modernist graphic precision

### Memphis Design

**What it is**: 1980s Italian postmodern movement led by Ettore Sottsass. Deliberate violation of modernist design rules — clashing colors, bold patterns, cheap materials (plastic laminate) in luxury contexts. Described as "a shotgun wedding between Bauhaus and Fisher-Price."

**Visual effect**: Loud, playful, chaotic. Clashing colors that violate harmony rules — hot pink next to lime green next to electric blue. Bold geometric patterns — squiggles, zigzags, triangles, dots. Asymmetric shapes that reference no particular tradition. Terrazzo patterns. The look is aggressively anti-serious and anti-minimalist. Reads as 1980s, postmodern, playful, and deliberately kitschy.

**Pairs well with**:
- Clashing/neon colors — pink, cyan, yellow, lime, coral simultaneously
- Bold geometric patterns — squiggles, dots, zigzags, terrazzo
- Asymmetric/irregular shapes — nothing is aligned or balanced
- Laminate/plastic textures — cheap materials styled expensively
- Postmodern irony — the "bad taste" is deliberate
- 1980s nostalgia — Memphis IS the 80s design aesthetic

**Conflicts with**:
- Minimalism — Memphis is maximalist
- Swiss/International Style — Memphis rejects the grid
- Neutral/muted palettes — Memphis is loud
- Seriousness — Memphis is playful and ironic
- Good taste (traditionally defined) — Memphis deliberately offends it

**Example combination**: Memphis + clashing neon colors + geometric patterns + terrazzo texture + asymmetric shapes → 80s maximalism, squiggles and zigzags, pink/cyan/yellow chaos, playful postmodern energy

### Brutalist (Graphic Design)

**What it is**: Web and graphic design inspired by Brutalist architecture — raw, unpolished, function-over-form. In graphic design: crude typography, stark layouts, no decoration, monochromatic or limited color, exposed structure. A reaction against polished, corporate design.

**Visual effect**: Raw, stark, confrontational. Large crude type, often monospaced or industrial. Black and white or very limited color. No gradients, no rounded corners, no softness. The design looks unfinished or deliberately rough. Reads as anti-establishment, punk, intellectual, or deliberately ugly.

**Pairs well with**:
- Raw concrete texture — the architectural reference
- Monospaced/industrial typography — Courier, monospace, stencil fonts
- Black and white — or black and one harsh accent color
- Hard edges — no curves, no softness
- Exposed grid/structure — the layout mechanics are visible
- Large blocks of text — type as mass, not decoration

**Conflicts with**:
- Polished/corporate design — Brutalism rejects polish
- Soft/rounded forms — everything is angular and hard
- Warm/inviting palettes — Brutalism is cold
- Decorative elements — stripped away entirely

**Example combination**: Brutalist design + raw concrete background + large monospaced type + black and white + hard edges → stark, confrontational, deliberately ugly, anti-corporate, raw

### Art Deco (Graphic)

**What it is**: The graphic design expression of Art Deco — geometric poster design, luxury typography, gold/chrome accents, symmetrical layouts. The era of the great poster designers. See Art Movements for the broader movement.

**Visual effect**: Geometric elegance in 2D. Sunburst radiating lines, stepped/zigzag borders, stylized figures with elongated proportions. Custom display typography with geometric letterforms. Gold, black, cream, and jewel tones. Symmetrical, centered layouts. Reads as vintage luxury, Jazz Age glamour, and sophisticated restraint.

**Pairs well with**:
- Geometric display typography — custom, angular, decorative letterforms
- Gold + black + cream — the classic Art Deco graphic palette
- Sunburst/radiating lines — the movement's signature motif
- Symmetrical/centered layouts — formal and balanced
- Elongated figures — stylized human forms with impossible proportions
- Beveled/embossed effects — dimensional typography

**Conflicts with**:
- Asymmetric/dynamic layouts — Art Deco graphic design is centered
- Sans-serif modernism — Art Deco type is decorative
- Casual/informal tone — too formal and polished
- Photographic imagery — Art Deco prefers illustration

### Psychedelic

**What it is**: 1960s-70s visual style from counterculture and rock poster design. Distorted lettering, vibrant clashing colors, optical illusions, Art Nouveau influences, organic flowing forms. San Francisco poster scene: Wes Wilson, Victor Moscoso.

**Visual effect**: Hallucinatory, vibrating, overwhelming. Colors clash at maximum saturation — orange against purple, magenta against green. Letterforms melt, warp, and flow into organic shapes. Patterns swirl and pulse. Optical effects make the image seem to move. Reads as counterculture, psychedelic experience, and 1960s rock and roll.

**Pairs well with**:
- Maximum saturation clashing colors — vibrating complementary pairs
- Distorted/melting letterforms — type becomes organic shape
- Art Nouveau flowing lines — direct influence on the style
- Optical patterns — moiré, concentric circles, pulsing fields
- Organic/flowing forms — nothing is angular or rigid
- Music/concert poster context — the native format

**Conflicts with**:
- Legibility — psychedelic type is notoriously hard to read
- Minimalism — maximum visual density
- Corporate/professional — too chaotic and countercultural
- Geometric/rigid grids — everything flows and melts

**Example combination**: Psychedelic style + flowing distorted type + vibrating orange/purple + organic swirls + concert poster format → hallucinatory, pulsing, lettering melting into patterns, 1960s counterculture energy

### Y2K / Early Internet

**What it is**: Late 1990s-early 2000s digital aesthetic. Translucent plastics, chrome/3D text, lens flares, gradient backgrounds, bubble shapes, early web design elements. The visual optimism of the dot-com era.

**Visual effect**: Shiny, translucent, futuristic-in-a-dated-way. Chrome 3D text with lens flares. Translucent colored plastic (iMac G3 aesthetic). Bubble and blob shapes. Gradient backgrounds (often blue-to-purple). Pixel fonts. The look is simultaneously futuristic and nostalgic — it represents a future that was imagined but never arrived.

**Pairs well with**:
- Chrome/metallic 3D text — beveled, reflective, lens-flared
- Translucent colored plastic — blue, green, orange, transparent
- Gradient backgrounds — blue to purple, silver to white
- Bubble/blob shapes — organic but synthetic
- Pixel fonts — early web typography
- Lens flares and sparkle effects — added to everything

**Conflicts with**:
- Flat/minimal design — Y2K is maximal and textured
- Natural/organic materials — everything is plastic and chrome
- Muted palettes — Y2K is shiny and vivid
- Contemporary design trends — Y2K is specifically dated (which is the appeal)

**Example combination**: Y2K + chrome 3D text + translucent blue plastic + gradient background + lens flares → shiny, optimistic, early-internet futurism, bubble aesthetic, nostalgic tech glamour

### Flat Design

**What it is**: 2010s digital design movement — no gradients, no shadows, no skeuomorphism. Solid color blocks, simple geometric shapes, sans-serif type. Microsoft Metro / Apple iOS 7 / Google Material Design. A reaction against skeuomorphic "realistic" interfaces.

**Visual effect**: Clean, simple, colorful. Solid flat colors, no depth simulation. Simple iconography. Bold sans-serif type. Whitespace. The design feels accessible, modern, and uncluttered. The most dominant current design language for apps, websites, and interfaces.

**Pairs well with**:
- Solid/bright color blocks — no gradients, no textures
- Simple geometric shapes — circles, rectangles, triangles
- Clean sans-serif typography — the interface typeface aesthetic
- Whitespace — generous spacing, breathing room
- Simple iconography — flat, two-tone icons
- Digital/UI context — the native environment

**Conflicts with**:
- Textured/dimensional surfaces — flat is deliberately 2D
- Ornamental detail — stripped away
- Photorealistic rendering — too dimensional
- Vintage/retro aesthetics — flat design is modern

### Neubrutalism / Neo-Brutalism

**What it is**: 2020s web/graphic design trend borrowing from Brutalism but softer — bold black borders, bright accent colors, raw/exposed UI elements, visible shadows, almost cartoonish. Figma, Gumroad, and many indie web products use this style.

**Visual effect**: Bold, playful, slightly raw. Heavy black borders/outlines around everything. Bright accent colors (yellow, pink, blue) against white or off-white. Drop shadows that look deliberately offset and chunky. Rounded corners mixed with hard edges. Reads as indie, approachable, design-aware, and contemporary.

**Pairs well with**:
- Heavy black outlines/borders — the defining visual element
- Bright accent colors — yellow, hot pink, electric blue
- Chunky offset drop shadows — deliberately visible and playful
- Sans-serif bold typography — clean but heavy
- White/off-white backgrounds — lets the bold elements pop
- Card-based layouts — distinct bordered sections

**Conflicts with**:
- Polished/corporate smoothness — Neubrutalism has rough edges
- Subtle/minimal design — too bold and chunky
- Gradient-heavy design — Neubrutalism uses flat color
- Classic Brutalism — Neubrutalism is playful, not confrontational

**Example combination**: Neubrutalism + heavy black borders + bright yellow accents + chunky shadows + bold type → indie web aesthetic, playful but bold, visible structure, contemporary and approachable

### Grunge Design (90s)

**What it is**: Raw, distressed graphic design from the grunge music era. Anti-corporate, DIY, deliberately ugly. The visual language of zines, alternative music, and Gen X disaffection.

**Visual effect**: Distressed textures, torn paper edges, ink splatter, xerox artifacts, dirty typewriter fonts. Muted/muddy palette — ochre, brown, black, olive. Layered grit. Photocopied photos with degraded halftone. The design looks like it was made on a broken copier in a basement.

**Pairs well with**:
- Distressed textures — torn paper, ink splatter, coffee stains
- Dirty typewriter/monospace fonts — courier, distressed type
- Muted/muddy palette — browns, blacks, olives, ochre
- Dadaism — shared collage chaos
- Punk zine aesthetic — shared DIY rebellion
- 1990s music culture — Nirvana, Soundgarden, Pearl Jam

**Conflicts with**:
- Swiss Design — too clean and ordered
- Flat Design — too polished
- Corporate Memphis — the exact target of grunge's rebellion
- Glassmorphism — too sleek

**Example combination**: Grunge design + torn paper collage + xerox texture + dirty typewriter type + muted palette → 90s zine energy, deliberately degraded, anti-corporate, raw

### Mid-Century Modern Illustration

**What it is**: 1950s-60s illustration from advertising's golden age. Simplified forms, limited palettes, textured printing, atomic-age motifs. Key artists: Charley Harper, Mary Blair (Disney), Paul Rand, Saul Bass, Miroslav Sasek.

**Visual effect**: Forms reduced to essential geometric shapes but retaining charm. Limited palette — often 2-4 colors plus black. Visible print texture (grain, halftone, slight misregistration). Atomic-age motifs: starbursts, boomerang shapes, kidney forms. Warm, optimistic, effortlessly cool.

**Pairs well with**:
- Limited palette (2-4 colors) — constraint as aesthetic
- Geometric simplification — Charley Harper's minimal wildlife
- Visible print texture — grain, halftone, offset
- Toned/kraft paper ground — the background participates
- Flat Design — modern descendant
- Risograph — shared spot-color, textured quality

**Conflicts with**:
- Photorealism — too simplified
- Dark/moody atmosphere — mid-century is optimistic
- Digital cleanness — the charm is analog texture
- Complex/busy compositions — mid-century is edited and clean

**Example combination**: Mid-century modern + woodland animals + 3-color palette + geometric simplification + print texture → Charley Harper energy, animals as essential shapes, warm limited color, retro print quality

### Corporate Memphis

**What it is**: The ubiquitous flat illustration style of tech companies (2018-2023). Big limbs, tiny heads, abstract diverse humans in cheerful scenarios. Used by Google, Facebook, Slack, Spotify, and dozens of others. Named ironically after Memphis Design.

**Visual effect**: Flat bright colors, disproportionate figures (long noodle arms, small heads), simple geometric shapes. No outlines or thin outlines. Cheerful and bland. Abstract skin tones (purple, blue, green people). Minimal backgrounds. The visual equivalent of corporate optimism.

**Pairs well with**:
- Flat Design — the parent aesthetic
- Bright/friendly palette — the corporate-approved rainbow
- Simple geometric shapes — everything is rounded rectangles and circles
- Tech company context — the native habitat

**Conflicts with**:
- Anything with personality — Grunge, Dadaism, Gothic, Pre-Raphaelite
- Gritty/raw aesthetics — Corporate Memphis is sterile
- Photorealism — too abstract
- Strong artistic voice — Corporate Memphis is designed to be generic

### Glassmorphism

**What it is**: UI design trend using frosted glass effect, transparency, and depth. Popularized by Apple's Big Sur and continued in modern interface design.

**Visual effect**: Frosted/blurred glass panels with subtle translucency. Background color/imagery visible through the blur. Subtle borders (thin white at low opacity). Soft shadows. Vibrant gradient backgrounds. Floating layered cards. Reads as modern, premium, futuristic.

**Pairs well with**:
- Vibrant gradient backgrounds — the glass effect needs something to blur
- Vaporwave — gradient backgrounds, floating elements
- Minimalism — clean, sparse layout with the glass panels
- Cyberpunk UI — futuristic dashboard aesthetic
- Dark mode — frosted glass on dark backgrounds is striking

**Conflicts with**:
- Grunge — too clean and sleek
- Brutalist — too polished
- Risograph — too digital and smooth
- Heavy textures — glassmorphism needs smoothness

---

### Skeuomorphism

**What it is**: Design that mimics real-world materials and objects digitally. The pre-iOS 7 Apple aesthetic. Leather textures, wood grain, realistic shadows, stitched edges, beveled buttons. The opposite of flat design.

**Visual effect**: Realistic material simulation in UI — leather calendar apps, wood-grain shelving for books, metal toggle switches, paper textures for notes. Drop shadows, gradients, and reflections create faux-3D depth. Reads as tactile, familiar, slightly dated (the 2007-2013 era).

**Pairs well with**:
- Steampunk — shared love of material simulation
- Dark Academia — texture richness
- Retro/nostalgic UI — the pre-flat era
- Detailed surface textures — leather, wood, metal, paper

**Conflicts with**:
- Flat Design — the literal opposite and its replacement
- Minimalism — too detailed and textured
- Swiss Design — too ornamental
- Neubrutalism — different philosophy entirely

---

### Kinetic Typography

**What it is**: Design where typography IS the visual element — oversized, stretched, compressed, layered, animated. Text as architecture and texture, not just communication.

**Visual effect**: Oversized letterforms dominating the frame. Stretched/compressed type. Letters as architectural elements. Text on curved paths. Overlapping transparent type layers. Type as texture. The words are the design.

**Pairs well with**:
- Swiss Design — shared typography focus
- Futurism — shared dynamism
- Brutalist — bold type as primary element
- Constructivism — type as political/graphic force
- Motion graphics — kinetic type in motion is the natural expression

**Conflicts with**:
- Image-heavy design — kinetic type replaces imagery
- Cottagecore — too graphic and industrial
- Ornamental styles — type is raw, not decorated
