# Illustration Styles

Drawing, painting, and printmaking techniques — each producing a distinct visual character. In prompt composition, referencing an illustration style shifts the output from photographic toward hand-crafted, and the specific style controls the look: clean lines, loose paint, bold cuts, or digital precision.

---

### Ligne Claire (Clear Line)

**What it is**: Belgian comic art style pioneered by Hergé (Tintin). Uniform-weight outlines, flat color fills, no hatching or crosshatching. Shadows are minimal or absent. Cartoonish characters against detailed, realistic backgrounds. Term coined by Joost Swarte in 1977.

**Visual effect**: Clean, readable, precise. Every element is defined by a consistent black outline filled with flat color. No tonal gradation within shapes. The simplicity is deceptive — the clarity requires extreme discipline. Reads as European comic art, precise, and deliberate.

**Pairs well with**:
- Flat/solid color fills — no gradients within shapes
- Detailed architectural backgrounds — realistic settings, simplified characters
- Clean black outlines — uniform weight, no variation
- Limited but vivid palette — each color is distinct and unblended
- Narrative/sequential context — comics, storyboards

**Conflicts with**:
- Painterly texture — ligne claire is anti-texture
- Atmospheric depth — the flat color prevents atmospheric perspective
- Photorealistic rendering — too simplified
- Heavy shadow/contrast — the style minimizes tonal variation

**Example combination**: Ligne claire + urban street scene + flat color + uniform outlines + detailed architecture → Tintin-esque clarity, buildings precisely rendered, characters simplified, clean and readable

**Prompt cues**: ligne claire comic style; clean uniform black outlines; flat color with no shading

### Woodcut / Linocut

**What it is**: Relief printmaking — image carved into wood (or linoleum), inked, and pressed onto paper. The carved-away areas print white, the raised surface prints black (or the ink color). One of the oldest printmaking techniques (China, ~200 AD; Europe, ~1400).

**Visual effect**: Bold, high-contrast, graphic. Strong black-and-white opposition with minimal gray tones. Visible texture from the wood grain (woodcut) or the gouge marks (linocut). Lines are bold and slightly irregular — the medium resists fine detail. Reads as handmade, historical, folk art, or deliberately raw.

**Pairs well with**:
- High contrast black and white — the natural output of the medium
- Bold/simple compositions — fine detail is difficult to carve
- Visible tool marks — gouge textures, wood grain
- Limited color (if multi-block) — each color is a separate printing
- Folk art / traditional context — the medium's cultural home
- Strong silhouettes — the relief process favors clear shapes

**Conflicts with**:
- Smooth gradients — the medium produces discrete tones, not gradations
- Fine detail — the carving tool limits resolution
- Photorealism — too graphic and simplified
- Digital cleanness — woodcut is inherently textured and handmade

**Example combination**: Woodcut style + bold black and white + landscape + visible wood grain texture + simple composition → dramatic contrast, handmade feel, graphic simplicity, folk art character

**Prompt cues**: woodcut print; bold black and white carved lines; visible gouge marks and grain texture

### Watercolor

**What it is**: Pigment suspended in water applied to paper. Transparent — light passes through the pigment and reflects off the white paper beneath, creating luminosity. Wet-in-wet (paint on wet paper) produces soft bleeds; wet-on-dry produces hard edges. The paper texture is always visible.

**Landmark techniques**: **Winslow Homer's ocean scenes** — wet-in-wet washes for sea and sky creating atmospheric depth, then sharp wet-on-dry strokes for wave crests and rocks. The tension between soft atmospheric washes and crisp detail strokes is his signature. **J.M.W. Turner** — watercolor pushed to abstraction, entire compositions built from translucent color washes with minimal drawing. **John Singer Sargent's watercolors** — bravura speed and confidence, each stroke placed once (no reworking), white paper preserved for highlights with surgical precision.

**Visual effect**: Luminous, transparent, spontaneous. Colors glow because the white paper shines through. Edges range from soft/diffused (wet-in-wet) to crisp (wet-on-dry). Pigment pools and blooms create organic, unpredictable textures. Reads as delicate, atmospheric, and hand-crafted.

**Pairs well with**:
- Visible paper texture — the grain shows through the paint
- Soft/diffused edges — bleeding, blooming watercolor effects
- Limited palette — watercolor is strongest with restrained color choices
- Botanical/nature subjects — the traditional watercolor genre
- Light/airy compositions — the transparency keeps things bright
- Atmospheric effects — fog, mist, sky gradations

**Conflicts with**:
- Opaque/heavy coverage — watercolor is transparent
- Dark/moody palettes — the medium trends light and luminous
- Hard/graphic edges everywhere — some softness is inherent
- Photorealism — the medium is too loose (hyperrealist watercolor exists but is rare)

**Example combination**: Watercolor + botanical illustration + visible paper texture + soft wet-in-wet backgrounds + precise wet-on-dry details → luminous flowers, soft atmospheric wash behind, paper grain visible, delicate and handmade

**Prompt cues**: watercolor painting; soft pigment blooms and wet bleeding edges; transparent layered washes; textured cold-press paper

### Oil Painting

**What it is**: Pigment in oil medium (linseed, walnut, poppy) on canvas or panel. Slow-drying, allowing extended blending and reworking. The dominant medium of Western painting from the 15th century onward.

**Landmark techniques**: **Rembrandt's layering** — dark ground, transparent glazes building luminosity, opaque highlights applied last (the light literally sits on top of the darkness). **Van Gogh's impasto** — paint applied so thickly (sometimes directly from the tube) that it casts real shadows on the canvas surface, creating a textural relief that catches light. **Vermeer's camera obscura softness** — edges so soft they appear photographic, smooth invisible brushwork, light falling with optical precision. **Sargent's bravura brushwork** — single loaded-brush strokes that simultaneously describe form, light, and material in one gesture.

**Visual effect**: Rich, deep, luminous. Colors have depth from the oil medium. Brushwork ranges from invisible glazes (Old Masters) to heavily textured impasto (Van Gogh). Reads as serious, classical, and authoritative.

**Pairs well with**:
- Visible brushstrokes / impasto — thick paint catching light
- Rich/deep color — the oil medium produces saturated, deep tones
- Classical subjects — portraiture, landscape, still life
- Chiaroscuro — oil painting's tonal range is unmatched
- Canvas texture — the weave visible through or alongside the paint
- Warm color — oil paintings tend warm due to the medium's amber quality

**Conflicts with**:
- Flat/graphic rendering — oil painting is dimensional and textured
- Digital cleanness — the medium is inherently physical
- Quick/spontaneous feeling — oil painting implies time and deliberation
- Pastel/washed-out palettes — oil color is too rich (unless deliberately thinned)

**Example combination**: Oil painting + impasto brushstrokes + portrait + warm light + dark background → rich tonal depth, visible paint texture catching light, classical luminosity, Old Master quality

**Prompt cues**: oil painting; thick visible brushstrokes and impasto; rich blended color with depth; canvas weave showing through

### Ink Wash / Sumi-e

**What it is**: East Asian painting technique using black ink (sumi) diluted to various concentrations on rice paper or silk. A single medium produces the full tonal range from deep black to pale gray. Rooted in Zen Buddhism — the brush stroke is a meditation.

**Visual effect**: Monochromatic (black ink only), atmospheric, minimal. The ink bleeds and diffuses on the absorbent paper, creating soft edges. A single brushstroke conveys form with maximum economy. Empty space (ma) is as important as the painted areas. Reads as contemplative, restrained, and ancient.

**Pairs well with**:
- Monochromatic black/gray — the medium is single-color
- Negative space / ma — emptiness is compositional
- Minimal brushstrokes — each stroke must count
- Nature subjects — bamboo, mountains, water, birds
- Atmospheric perspective — distant mountains fade to pale gray
- Calligraphy — text and image share the same brush

**Conflicts with**:
- Full color — sumi-e is monochromatic
- Dense/busy compositions — the style requires breathing room
- Sharp/defined edges everywhere — ink bleeds on absorbent paper
- Western compositional rules — sumi-e follows different spatial logic

**Example combination**: Sumi-e + mountain landscape + minimal brushstrokes + vast empty space + ink gradation → mountains emerging from mist, a few decisive strokes, most of the paper empty, contemplative silence

**Prompt cues**: black ink wash painting; graded washes from pale grey to black; loose expressive brush strokes; large areas of bare paper

### Ukiyo-e (Japanese Woodblock)

**What it is**: Japanese woodblock printing tradition (17th-19th century). Bold outlines, flat color areas, asymmetric composition, limited palette from multi-block printing. Subjects: landscapes, portraits, nature, theater. Key artists: Hokusai, Hiroshige, Utamaro.

**Visual effect**: Flat color bounded by bold black lines. No modeling or shading — color is uniform within each area. Compositions are asymmetric and often cropped unexpectedly. Strong sense of pattern and decorative surface. Colors are limited by the printing process but vivid within that constraint. Reads as distinctly Japanese, decorative, and graphic.

**Pairs well with**:
- Bold black outlines — the defining visual element
- Flat color fills — no gradation within color areas
- Asymmetric/unexpected composition — Japanese compositional sensibility
- Nature subjects — waves, mountains, cherry blossoms, birds
- Limited but vivid palette — blues, reds, greens, earth tones
- Pattern and repetition — waves, clouds, rain rendered as pattern

**Conflicts with**:
- Photorealistic rendering — ukiyo-e is flat and graphic
- Smooth tonal gradation — the woodblock process produces flat areas
- Western perspective — ukiyo-e uses its own spatial logic
- Muted/desaturated color — the prints are vivid

**Example combination**: Ukiyo-e style + crashing wave + bold outlines + flat blue and white + asymmetric composition → Hokusai's Great Wave energy, flat color bounded by bold lines, decorative pattern, graphic power

**Prompt cues**: traditional woodblock print; flat color inside bold black outlines; decorative patterned surfaces; asymmetric cropped composition

### Vector / Flat Illustration

**What it is**: Digital illustration using mathematical curves (Bézier paths) rather than pixels. Clean edges at any scale. Solid color fills, minimal or no gradients. The language of modern digital illustration, iconography, and infographics.

**Visual effect**: Perfectly clean, scalable, geometric. No texture, no brushwork, no imperfection. Colors are flat and solid. Lines are mathematically smooth. Reads as digital, modern, corporate-friendly, and accessible. The dominant style for app illustrations, brand assets, and explainer graphics.

**Pairs well with**:
- Flat color — no gradients, no texture
- Clean geometric shapes — circles, rectangles, smooth curves
- Limited/branded palettes — corporate color systems
- Simple subjects — people, objects, scenes simplified to essentials
- Flat design / UI context — the native environment
- Scalability — works at icon size or billboard size

**Conflicts with**:
- Handmade/textured quality — vector is too clean
- Photographic reference — too abstract and simplified
- Atmospheric depth — flat color prevents depth
- Emotional complexity — the simplification limits expression

**Prompt cues**: flat vector illustration; clean geometric shapes; solid flat colors; smooth perfect lines

### Pencil / Graphite

**What it is**: Drawing with graphite pencil on paper. The most fundamental drawing medium. Tonal range from light gray to near-black through pressure and layering. Visible paper texture. Line quality ranges from precise to gestural.

**Visual effect**: Intimate, direct, personal. The artist's hand is always visible. Tones are built through hatching, crosshatching, blending, or layering. Paper grain shows through. The graphite has a subtle metallic sheen at heavy applications. Reads as honest, studied, and handmade.

**Pairs well with**:
- Detailed rendering — graphite excels at fine detail
- Portraiture — the tonal range captures facial dimension
- Hatching/crosshatching texture — the building blocks of pencil tone
- Visible paper texture — white or toned paper showing through
- Sketch/study quality — the medium implies process, not finish
- Monochrome — graphite is inherently gray-scale

**Conflicts with**:
- Vivid color — graphite is monochromatic (unless colored pencil)
- Bold/graphic impact — graphite is subtle
- Clean/digital precision — pencil has inherent irregularity
- Flat/uniform tone — graphite builds tone gradually

**Prompt cues**: graphite pencil drawing; fine hatched shading in grey tones; soft smudged blending; textured drawing paper

---

### Digital Painting

**What it is**: Painting in software (Photoshop, Procreate, Clip Studio) using a stylus/tablet. Mimics traditional painting but with digital tools — layers, undo, infinite color mixing. Can imitate any traditional medium or create hybrid styles.

**Visual effect**: Varies hugely depending on the artist's approach. Can mimic oil painting (textured brushstrokes, rich color), watercolor (soft bleeds), or create a distinctive digital look (smooth blending, perfect gradients, no paper texture). The "concept art" look — smooth, polished, cinematic digital painting — is the most recognizable variant.

**Pairs well with**:
- Concept art / entertainment design — the industry standard medium
- Cinematic lighting — digital painting can simulate any light setup
- Character design — turnarounds, expressions, poses
- Environment/world building — landscapes, cityscapes, interiors
- Any color palette — unlimited color mixing

**Conflicts with**:
- Physical medium texture — no real paper grain or paint thickness (unless simulated)
- Imperfection as character — digital tools trend toward polish
- Hand-crafted authenticity — the medium is inherently digital

**Prompt cues**: digital painting; smooth blended brushwork; polished cinematic rendering

### Risograph / Screen Print

**What it is**: Layered printing with limited spot colors (1-3 inks), each applied as a separate layer. Risograph is a modern stencil duplicator; screen printing is manual. Slight misregistration between layers is characteristic. Each ink is a flat, semi-transparent color.

**Visual effect**: Graphic, layered, textured. Limited palette (each color is a separate ink/pass). Colors overlap to create secondary hues. Slight misalignment between layers adds organic imperfection. The ink has a distinct texture — slightly speckled, grainy, and tactile. Reads as indie, zine culture, handmade, and contemporary craft.

**Pairs well with**:
- Limited palette (2-3 colors) — the process constrains color
- Overprinting / color overlap — two inks combining into a third color
- Slight misregistration — the charming imperfection of manual printing
- Flat/graphic compositions — the inks print flat
- Zine / indie publishing — the cultural context
- Grainy/textured ink — the risograph's speckled quality

**Conflicts with**:
- Full color / photographic — too many colors for the process
- Smooth gradients — the inks print flat
- Digital precision — the charm is in the imperfection
- Large editions — risograph is small-batch by nature

**Example combination**: Risograph + two colors (fluorescent pink + dark blue) + slight misregistration + flat graphic illustration → layered spot colors overlapping to create purple where they meet, grainy ink texture, indie zine energy

**Prompt cues**: risograph print; limited overlapping ink colors; misregistered color layers; grainy speckled ink texture

### Collage

**What it is**: Composition made from cut/torn fragments of existing imagery — photographs, magazines, paper, fabric, found materials. Dada, Surrealism, and punk culture all used collage extensively. Physical or digital.

**Visual effect**: Layered, fragmented, juxtaposed. Different textures, scales, and source materials coexist. Visible edges where pieces are cut or torn. Scale and context shift abruptly — a giant eye next to tiny buildings. Reads as rebellious, surreal, DIY, or postmodern depending on context.

**Pairs well with**:
- Mixed textures — photographic + drawn + typographic fragments
- Surreal juxtaposition — impossible combinations from real sources
- Visible cut/torn edges — the construction is part of the aesthetic
- Punk/zine context — collage is the DIY art form
- Layered composition — depth through stacking
- Found imagery — the materials come from elsewhere

**Conflicts with**:
- Visual unity — collage is deliberately disunified
- Clean/smooth surfaces — the edges and layers are visible
- Single-medium consistency — collage mixes everything
- Minimal compositions — collage tends busy

**Prompt cues**: mixed media collage; cut and torn paper pieces; clashing textures and scales layered together

### Gouache

**What it is**: Opaque watercolor — pigment with gum arabic mixed with white for opacity. Unlike transparent watercolor, gouache covers the surface completely. Matte finish. Used extensively in illustration, animation backgrounds (Disney, Ghibli prep), and commercial art.

**Visual effect**: Matte, velvety, opaque. Colors sit on the surface rather than glowing through. Flat, even coverage with visible brushstrokes at close range. Colors dry slightly lighter. Reads as illustrative, warm, handmade, slightly retro.

**Pairs well with**:
- Matte/flat color — the natural output
- Children's book subjects — the medium's commercial home
- Mid-century illustration — gouache was the industry standard
- Warm, muted palette — gouache dries chalky
- Nature/botanical subjects — precise but painterly

**Conflicts with**:
- Glossy/reflective surfaces — aggressively matte
- Transparent/luminous effects — gouache is opaque
- Deep saturated darks — the chalk content lightens everything
- Photorealism — too painterly and flat

**Example combination**: Gouache + forest scene + matte flat color + warm palette + visible brushwork → velvety storybook quality, chalky but rich, handmade warmth

**Prompt cues**: gouache painting; flat opaque matte color; crisp painted edges; visible brush texture

---

### Charcoal Drawing

**What it is**: Drawing with burned wood (vine, compressed, or pencil charcoal) on textured paper. Extremely soft, producing deep velvety blacks. Easily smudged and blended. One of the oldest drawing materials.

**Visual effect**: Dark, dramatic, atmospheric. Velvety blacks graphite cannot match. Soft, smoky edges from blending. Paper texture highly visible. Tonal range from near-white (erased) to absolute black (compressed). Reads as raw, emotional, expressive, classical.

**Pairs well with**:
- Chiaroscuro — charcoal excels at dramatic light/dark
- Portraiture and figure drawing — the life drawing standard
- Smudged/blended tones — the medium's signature softness
- Visible paper grain — charcoal reveals surface texture
- Atmospheric/moody subjects — fog, smoke, shadow

**Conflicts with**:
- Clean precise lines — charcoal is inherently soft
- Color — pure monochrome
- Fine detail — the medium is too broad
- Digital precision — maximally physical

**Example combination**: Charcoal + portrait + chiaroscuro + smudged background + visible paper grain → deep velvety blacks, smoky blended tones, classical life-drawing intensity

**Prompt cues**: charcoal drawing; deep velvety blacks and smudged greys; rough expressive strokes; grainy paper texture

---

### Pastel (Chalk & Oil)

**What it is**: Two distinct media. **Chalk/soft pastels**: nearly pure pigment with minimal binder. Powdery, blendable, luminous. Degas made them famous. **Oil pastels**: pigment in oil/wax binder. Creamier, resist blending, thick visible strokes.

**Visual effect**: Chalk — soft, powdery, luminous. Intense saturated color from nearly pure pigment. Smooth velvety gradations. Paper color shows through (toned paper traditional). Oil — waxy, thick, textured. Bold stroke marks, colors layer rather than mix. Both read as tactile and handmade.

**Pairs well with**:
- Toned/colored paper — the paper participates in the image
- Intense saturated color — pure pigment delivers vivid hues
- Impressionistic subjects — landscapes, dancers, atmospheric scenes
- Soft/diffused edges (chalk) — blended pastel is inherently soft
- Bold stroke marks (oil) — thick, waxy texture

**Conflicts with**:
- Hard clean edges — pastels blur everything
- Fine detail — too soft and broad
- Photorealism — too textured
- Digital cleanness — the medium is maximally physical

**Prompt cues**: soft pastel drawing; powdery chalky strokes; velvety saturated color; toned paper showing through

---

### Etching / Engraving (Intaglio)

**What it is**: Lines incised into a metal plate (copper, zinc). Etching: acid bites exposed metal through a wax ground. Engraving: burin cuts directly into metal. Plate is inked, wiped, pressed onto damp paper. Rembrandt, Dürer, Goya, Piranesi.

**Visual effect**: Extremely fine, precise lines on light ground. Tonal range built through hatching, crosshatching, and stipple. Slight plate tone (residual ink) creates warmth. Embossed plate mark at edges. Reads as classical, precise, intellectual, archival.

**Pairs well with**:
- Fine crosshatching — the fundamental building block
- Warm cream/sepia paper — traditional printing surface
- Architectural subjects — Piranesi's etchings define the genre
- Classical/historical context — maps, banknotes, scientific plates
- High detail — the medium rewards meticulous work

**Conflicts with**:
- Bold graphic simplicity — etching is about fine detail
- Color — typically single-ink monochrome
- Soft/blurry edges — lines are sharp and defined
- Loose/gestural marks — the medium is precise

**Example combination**: Etching + architectural interior + fine crosshatching + warm sepia paper → Piranesi energy, obsessive line detail, tonal depth from hatching density

**Prompt cues**: etching; fine precise engraved lines; tone built from hatching and crosshatching; warm plate tone

---

### Scratchboard

**What it is**: White clay-coated board covered with black ink. Artist scratches into the surface with a sharp tool, revealing white beneath. Subtractive — drawing white on black by removing material.

**Visual effect**: White lines on black ground. Extremely fine, precise lines. Pure black and white — no gray tones (unless partially scraped). Distinctively sharp, thin lines. Reads as dramatic, precise, editorial. The standard for wildlife illustration.

**Pairs well with**:
- High contrast black and white — the only two values
- Fine detail / texture — fur, feathers, scales rendered hair by hair
- Wildlife/nature subjects — the editorial illustration standard
- Dramatic lighting — light emerging from darkness
- Editorial / book illustration context

**Conflicts with**:
- Color — pure B&W medium
- Soft/blurry effects — every mark is sharp
- Light backgrounds — the ground is black
- Loose/gestural work — scratchboard rewards patience

**Example combination**: Scratchboard + owl portrait + fine white lines on black + detailed feather texture → individual feathers scratched in, dramatic, editorial wildlife quality

**Prompt cues**: scratchboard illustration; fine white lines scratched from black; stark black and white

---

### Stipple / Pointillism Technique

**What it is**: **Stippling** (ink): tone built entirely from dots, no lines. Used in scientific and editorial illustration. **Pointillism** (paint): Seurat/Signac Neo-Impressionist technique of colored dots that optically blend.

**Visual effect**: Stippling — tonal gradation through dot density. No lines at all. Precise, scientific, obsessively detailed. Pointillism — vibrant color from optical mixing of individual colored dots. Shimmering, luminous surface.

**Pairs well with**:
- Scientific/botanical subjects (stipple) — the traditional context
- Luminous color (pointillism) — optical mixing produces brightness
- High detail — dots render extremely fine gradations
- Natural subjects — landscapes, flora, fauna
- Matte paper texture — dots sit on the surface

**Conflicts with**:
- Clean flat color — dots create inherent texture
- Bold graphic lines — the point is no lines (stipple)
- Smooth blending — surface is granular at close range
- Speed — both techniques are painstaking

**Prompt cues**: stippled illustration; tone built entirely from dots; dense dot shading

### Cross-Hatching (as Style)

**What it is**: Building tone entirely through layers of parallel lines crossing at angles. Not just a shading technique but a complete illustration approach. Foundational to pen-and-ink, engraving, and editorial cartooning.

**Visual effect**: Dense, textured, linear. Every tonal value constructed from visible intersecting lines. Spacing and density control darkness. Reads as editorial, literary, pen-and-ink, classical. The New Yorker illustration tradition.

**Pairs well with**:
- Pen and ink — the natural home
- Editorial / literary illustration — the book illustration tradition
- Portraiture and figure work — cross-hatching models form powerfully
- Limited palette (black + one accent) — the editorial tradition

**Conflicts with**:
- Smooth tonal gradation — lines are always visible
- Painterly softness — inherently linear and crisp
- Photorealism — too graphic and constructed

**Prompt cues**: pen and ink crosshatching; tone from dense intersecting lines; fine linework

### Stained Glass Style

**What it is**: Illustration mimicking medieval and Art Nouveau stained glass windows. Bold black outlines (lead came) enclosing areas of luminous, saturated, translucent-looking color.

**Visual effect**: Luminous, jewel-toned, segmented. Thick black outlines divide the composition into discrete color cells. Colors appear to glow as if backlit. Reads as sacred, decorative, medieval, or Art Nouveau.

**Pairs well with**:
- Bold black outlines — the structural element
- Jewel-tone saturated color — ruby, sapphire, emerald, amber
- Backlit luminous quality — light passing through color
- Religious/sacred subjects — the original context
- Art Nouveau — shared organic flowing lines
- Symmetrical/radial composition — rose window structure

**Conflicts with**:
- Photorealism — too segmented
- Muted palettes — stained glass is saturated
- Subtle gradation — each cell is separate
- Loose/spontaneous marks — the leading is structural

**Prompt cues**: stained glass style; glowing jewel-toned color cells; thick black dividing lines; backlit luminous color

---

### Paper Cut / Kirigami Style

**What it is**: Illustration mimicking cut paper art. Flat shapes layered with shadows between planes. Cultural traditions worldwide: Chinese jianzhi, Mexican papel picado, Matisse's cutouts.

**Visual effect**: Flat shapes with hard edges, layered with subtle shadows between planes. No drawn lines — every form is a cut silhouette. Depth from layering, not perspective. Reads as crafted, folk art, or modern depending on execution.

**Pairs well with**:
- Flat single-color shapes — each piece is one paper color
- Layered depth with drop shadows — stacked planes
- Bold/simplified forms — detail limited by cutting
- Folk art / cultural motifs — global paper-cutting traditions
- Limited palette — each layer is one color

**Conflicts with**:
- Fine line detail — no lines, only cut shapes
- Tonal gradation — each piece is flat
- Texture within shapes — paper is uniform

**Example combination**: Paper cut + layered birds and flowers + subtle shadows between layers + limited palette → Matisse cutout energy, bold silhouettes, visible depth, handmade craft

**Prompt cues**: layered paper cut art; flat cut-paper shapes with crisp edges; soft shadows between paper layers

---

### Concept Art

**What it is**: Visual development illustration for entertainment — film, games, animation. Designed to communicate design ideas quickly. Typically digital painting with polished-but-efficient execution. Key names: Syd Mead, Ralph McQuarrie, Craig Mullins.

**Visual effect**: Cinematic, atmospheric, polished but efficient. Strong lighting design — rim lights, volumetric fog, bounced color. Compositions framed like film shots. Paint strokes are economical — detail where it matters, loose everywhere else. Reads as cinematic, professional, genre-fiction.

**Pairs well with**:
- Dramatic cinematic lighting — the defining quality
- Sci-fi/fantasy subjects — the genre home
- Digital painting medium — the industry standard
- Atmospheric effects — fog, particles, volumetric light
- Wide/epic compositions — establishing shots

**Conflicts with**:
- Traditional/handmade feel — concept art reads as digital
- Abstract subjects — concept art serves narrative design
- Folk art / naive aesthetics — too technically skilled

---

### Children's Book Illustration

**What it is**: Illustration for picture books (ages 2-8). Characterized by warmth, readability, emotional clarity. Spans many media (watercolor, gouache, digital, collage) but shares common principles. Key names: Beatrix Potter, Maurice Sendak, Eric Carle, Oliver Jeffers.

**Visual effect**: Warm, inviting, expressive. Large eyes and readable emotions on characters. Clear, uncluttered compositions. Warm colors. Visible medium texture. Scale plays with a child's perspective — things loom, adults viewed from below.

**Pairs well with**:
- Warm friendly palette — earth tones, soft primaries
- Expressive character faces — large eyes, clear emotions
- Whimsical subjects — animals, magical creatures
- Visible medium texture — watercolor, gouache, crayon
- Narrative context — the illustration tells a story

**Conflicts with**:
- Dark/scary atmospherics — defaults to warmth and safety
- Photorealism — too complex
- Abstract subjects — needs clear narrative
- Technical precision — warmth over accuracy

---

### Comic Book (American)

**What it is**: Superhero-dominated American tradition. Bold black outlines, dynamic anatomy, dramatic perspective. Heavy inking with brush and nib. Key names: Jack Kirby, Jim Lee, Frank Miller, Todd McFarlane.

**Visual effect**: Bold, dynamic, muscular. Heavy black outlines with variable line weight. Dramatic foreshortening and exaggerated anatomy. Saturated color with strong shadows. Speed lines, impact effects. Reads as heroic, kinetic, larger-than-life.

**Pairs well with**:
- Bold black inking — variable-weight brush lines
- Dynamic/exaggerated anatomy — heroic proportions
- Saturated bold color — primaries and secondaries
- Dramatic foreshortening — Kirby dynamism
- Action/superhero subjects — the native content
- Ben-Day dots (retro) or flat digital color (modern)

**Conflicts with**:
- Subtle/quiet compositions — the style is loud
- Soft/muted palette — too bold
- Minimalism — the style is maximal
- Photorealism — exaggerated by design

**Prompt cues**: comic book art; heavy black inks with bold outlines; saturated flat color; dynamic action lines

### Manga (as illustration)

**What it is**: Japanese comic art tradition. Clean precise linework, distinctive character design (large expressive eyes), screen tone for shading. Predominantly black and white. Vast genre range. Key names: Tezuka, Toriyama, CLAMP, Junji Ito.

**Production specifics**: Professional mangakas use **B4 manuscript paper** (~257x364mm) with printed safe/trim/bleed guides; doujinshi (self-published) artists use A4. Resolution: **600 DPI** standard for B&W with screen tone. Two primary pen nibs: **G-pen** (open slit, variable line thickness with pressure — used for character outlines and bold lines) and **maru-pen** (fine, thin lines — used for eyes, hair, and detailed backgrounds). **Screen tone**: adhesive sheets of dot patterns at various densities (typically 10-60% black), cut with X-acto and burnished onto the page. Demands firm, smooth paper — thin paper creates bubbles and uneven adhesion.

**Visual effect**: Clean, precise, expressive. Thinner, more uniform linework than American comics. Screen tone (mechanical dot patterns) for shading instead of rendered shadow. Backgrounds alternate between detailed environments and abstract emotional spaces. Characters' eyes carry enormous emotional weight.

**Pairs well with**:
- Clean precise linework — thinner than American comic inks
- Screen tone shading — mechanical dot patterns
- Large expressive eyes — the emotional anchor
- Black and white — the primary format
- Speed lines / emotional background effects — sparkles, petals, abstract

**Conflicts with**:
- Painterly texture — manga is crisp and clean
- Western color palettes — manga is primarily monochrome
- Loose/sketchy linework — manga lines are controlled
- Realistic proportions (usually) — deliberately stylized

**Prompt cues**: black and white manga illustration; clean thin linework; screentone dot shading

### Botanical / Scientific Illustration

**What it is**: Precise illustration of natural specimens combining artistic skill with scientific accuracy. Traditionally watercolor on vellum or paper. Key names: Maria Sibylla Merian, Pierre-Joseph Redouté, Ernst Haeckel, Audubon.

**Visual effect**: Precise, detailed, luminous, clinical. Every detail rendered with taxonomic accuracy — veins, petal structure, stamen count. White/cream background (specimen isolation). Fine brushwork. Reads as scientific, beautiful, authoritative, timeless.

**Pairs well with**:
- Watercolor medium — the traditional technique
- White/cream background — specimen isolation
- Extreme botanical detail — veins, pistils, root structure
- Scientific annotation — Latin names, measurements
- Multiple views — cross-section, detail, whole plant

**Conflicts with**:
- Loose/expressive painting — scientific illustration is controlled
- Environmental context — specimens are isolated
- Stylization — accuracy is paramount

**Example combination**: Botanical illustration + orchid + watercolor on cream paper + cross-section view + scientific annotation → Redouté precision, every structure rendered, luminous and authoritative

### Technical / Patent Illustration

**What it is**: Precise line drawings showing construction, assembly, or function of objects. Patent illustration follows strict guidelines: black on white, specific views, numbered callouts. Pure communication, no artistic expression.

**Visual effect**: Clean uniform-weight black lines on white. No shading except minimal hatching. Exploded views show components separated along assembly axes. Numbered callouts. Dashed lines for hidden features. Reads as engineering, institutional, objective.

**Pairs well with**:
- Clean black lines on white — standard format
- Exploded/assembly views — the signature diagram type
- Numbered callouts — parts identification
- Mechanical/industrial subjects — the natural content
- Multiple orthographic views — front, side, top, section

**Conflicts with**:
- Artistic expression — technical illustration is objective
- Color — typically monochrome
- Atmospheric effects — information only
- Organic subjects — suits manufactured objects

**Prompt cues**: patent drawing; clean uniform black lines on white; numbered callout labels; dashed hidden lines

### Graffiti / Street Art

**What it is**: Visual language of urban art. Graffiti: letterform-based (tags, throw-ups, wildstyle). Street art: broader (murals, stencils, wheat-paste). From 1970s NYC subway culture to global movement.

**Visual effect**: Bold, colorful, energetic. Exaggerated dimensional letterforms. Saturated, high-contrast color. Spray paint effects: soft overspray edges, hard stencil edges, drips and runs. Urban surface context (brick, concrete, metal). Reads as rebellious, urban, youth culture.

**Pairs well with**:
- Spray paint texture — overspray, drips, fades
- Bold saturated color — neon, primary, high contrast
- Urban surfaces — brick walls, concrete, metal shutters
- Dimensional letterforms — 3D block letters with shadows
- Characters alongside letters — cartoon figures, faces

**Conflicts with**:
- Clean/polished surfaces — graffiti needs urban grit
- Classical/formal composition — graffiti is anarchic
- Muted/pastel palettes — too bold and saturated

**Example combination**: Graffiti wildstyle + dimensional letters + spray paint on brick + neon colors + drips → NYC subway energy, interlocking letters, saturated color on gritty brick

**Prompt cues**: graffiti street art; spray paint with soft overspray and drips; bold saturated colors with thick outlines
