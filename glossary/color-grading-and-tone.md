# Color Grading & Tone

Color grading controls the emotional register of an image after capture. It's the difference between a photo that looks like a Tuesday afternoon and one that looks like a memory, a threat, or a dream. In prompt composition, color and tone descriptors set the mood before the subject is even described.

---

## Color Schemes

How colors relate to each other within the frame. These are compositional decisions about what colors appear and how they interact.

### Complementary Colors

**What it is**: Two colors opposite each other on the color wheel — red/green, blue/orange, yellow/purple. Maximum color contrast.

**Visual effect**: High visual tension and energy. Each color makes the other appear more vivid. The contrast is eye-catching and dynamic. The most common complementary pairing in cinema is **teal and orange** (skin tones are warm/orange, shadows and backgrounds are pushed toward teal), which became the dominant Hollywood color grade in the 2010s.

**Pairs well with**:
- High-energy/dynamic scenes — the contrast demands attention
- Fashion editorial — bold color contrast reads as stylish
- Teal and orange specifically — cinematic, commercially proven, flattering to skin
- Neon lighting — colored lights naturally create complementary zones
- Sunset/golden hour against blue sky — nature's complementary palette

**Conflicts with**:
- Harmonious/calm mood — complementary colors create tension, not peace
- Monochromatic palettes — the opposite approach
- Subtle/understated color — complementary is inherently loud

**Example combination**: Teal and orange grade + cinematic lighting + portrait + shallow depth of field → warm skin tones against cool teal shadows, Hollywood blockbuster color science

**Prompt cues**: complementary color palette; two opposing hues side by side; vivid color contrast

---

### Analogous Colors

**What it is**: Colors adjacent on the color wheel — blue/blue-green/green, red/orange/yellow. Minimal color contrast.

**Visual effect**: Harmonious, unified, cohesive. The image feels like it belongs to a single color family. Calm and pleasing without the tension of complementary contrast. The lack of color opposition creates a meditative, immersive quality.

**Pairs well with**:
- Calm/serene mood — the harmony soothes
- Nature/landscape — forests (green analogous), sunsets (warm analogous), ocean (blue analogous)
- Monochromatic-adjacent work — almost single-hue
- Muted/pastel palettes — analogous colors in low saturation

**Conflicts with**:
- High energy/contrast — analogous is too calm
- Subject separation — similar colors make foreground/background blend
- Bold graphic statements — not enough contrast

**Example combination**: Blue-green analogous palette + overcast seascape + muted tones → ocean, sky, and rocks all in the same cool color family, meditative, unified

**Prompt cues**: analogous color palette; neighboring hues from one color family; harmonious unified color

---

### Split Complementary

**What it is**: One base color paired with the two colors flanking its complement — e.g., blue paired with yellow-orange and red-orange instead of just orange.

**Visual effect**: High contrast like complementary, but more nuanced and less aggressive. The two accent colors create variety while still opposing the base. More sophisticated than straight complementary.

**Pairs well with**:
- Fashion/editorial — more interesting color tension than simple complementary
- Complex scenes with multiple color elements — the three-color structure organizes them
- Stylized/art-directed imagery — the deliberate palette reads as intentional

**Conflicts with**:
- Simplicity — three deliberate colors is more complex than two
- Natural/ungraded imagery — split complementary is a conscious choice

**Prompt cues**: split complementary palette; one base hue with two contrasting accents; balanced color contrast

---

### Triadic

**What it is**: Three colors equally spaced around the color wheel — red/yellow/blue (primary triad), orange/green/purple (secondary triad).

**Visual effect**: Vibrant, balanced, energetic. Each color gets equal weight. The balance prevents any single color from dominating. Reads as playful, graphic, or pop-art-influenced.

**Pairs well with**:
- Graphic design / pop art — bold, balanced color
- Children's/playful imagery — the vibrancy reads as fun
- Fashion with bold color blocking — each piece a different triad member
- Saturated palettes — triadic needs saturation to read

**Conflicts with**:
- Muted/desaturated palettes — triadic needs vibrancy
- Single-mood imagery — three colors pull in three directions
- Realism — nature rarely arranges itself in perfect triads

**Prompt cues**: triadic color palette; three evenly spaced bold hues; bright balanced pop-art color

---

### Monochromatic

**What it is**: Variations of a single hue — different saturations and values of the same color. A blue image with navy shadows and powder blue highlights is monochromatic.

**Visual effect**: Unified, immersive, stylized. The single-hue restriction creates a strong mood — blue monochrome feels cold, red feels intense, green feels organic. The viewer is bathed in the color. Form, texture, and light do the compositional work since color contrast is removed.

**Pairs well with**:
- Strong mood/atmosphere — the color dominates and sets the emotion
- Neon/colored gel lighting — a single color light source creates natural monochrome
- Graphic/stylized imagery — the restriction is a design choice
- B&W toning (sepia, cyanotype) — monochromatic by nature

**Conflicts with**:
- Color variety/contrast — monochromatic eliminates it
- Natural/realistic rendering — the world isn't one color
- Subject separation via color — everything is the same hue

**Example combination**: Blue monochromatic + nighttime + single cool light source + portrait → face in shades of blue, navy shadows, powder highlights, cold and atmospheric

**Prompt cues**: monochromatic palette; every tone a shade of one hue; image bathed in a single color

---

### Color Blocking

**What it is**: Large, flat areas of bold, contrasting solid color. Mondrian in photography. The design principle applied to the whole frame.

**Visual effect**: Graphic, bold, modern, fashion-forward. Strong visual impact. Flattens depth — the bold planes read as design rather than space. Reads as editorial, pop, confident.

**Pairs well with**:
- Minimalist composition — clean shapes of color
- Fashion photography — bold clothing against bold backgrounds
- Flat lighting — even illumination lets the color speak
- Pop art — shared bold flat color language
- Complementary or triadic color schemes — maximum contrast

**Conflicts with**:
- Photorealism — too graphic and designed
- Moody chiaroscuro — needs even light
- Natural environments — nature doesn't color-block
- Muted/desaturated palettes — needs vivid color

**Prompt cues**: color blocking; large flat planes of bold solid color; graphic hard-edged color areas

---

### Complementary Grade Combos (Beyond Teal & Orange)

Named complementary color grade pairings used in cinema:

- **Amber + Steel Blue** — warmer, less aggressive. Period dramas. The English Patient.
- **Magenta + Green** — unsettling, surreal. Horror, sci-fi. Spring Breakers, Refn films.
- **Gold + Deep Blue** — regal, epic. Fantasy, historical. Gladiator, Lord of the Rings.
- **Teal + Pink/Magenta** — vaporwave-adjacent. Drive, Miami Vice.
- **Yellow-Green + Purple** — toxic, alien. Alien franchise, Annihilation.
- **Copper + Slate** — industrial, restrained. Sicario, Prisoners.
- **Cream + Navy** — classic, clean, editorial. Fashion, lifestyle.

Each pairing creates a distinct emotional register. Specifying the combination in a prompt is more effective than just "cinematic color grade."

## Grading Approaches

Specific color grading styles and their visual character.

### Teal and Orange (Hollywood Grade)

**What it is**: The dominant color grade of 2010s blockbuster cinema. Push shadows and backgrounds toward teal/cyan, push skin tones and highlights toward warm orange/amber. Complementary color contrast.

**Visual effect**: Cinematic, commercial, polished. Skin pops against cool backgrounds. Faces look warm and alive. The overall image has a high-production-value sheen. So ubiquitous it's become the default "cinematic" look.

**Pairs well with**:
- Any scene with skin tones — the grade is designed around warm skin vs cool surroundings
- Action/blockbuster aesthetic — the association is baked in
- Three-point or dramatic lighting — the grade amplifies light/shadow color separation
- Modern commercial work — the most commercially safe color grade

**Conflicts with**:
- Naturalistic color — teal and orange is an obvious manipulation
- Vintage/film stock looks — too modern and digital
- Warm-overall or cool-overall palettes — T&O requires both temperatures

**Prompt cues**: teal and orange color grade; warm orange skin tones; teal-shifted shadows and backgrounds

---

### Desaturated / Muted

**What it is**: Overall color saturation reduced. Colors are present but quieted — grays, dusty pastels, faded tones. Not black and white, but moving toward it.

**Visual effect**: Somber, serious, introspective. The reduced color removes vibrancy and energy, creating a contemplative or melancholy quality. Common in drama, war films, and prestige television. David Fincher's grade (Se7en, Fight Club, Zodiac) is the reference point — green-tinted desaturation that feels oppressive and clinical.

**Pairs well with**:
- Drama/thriller — desaturation creates tension and seriousness
- Overcast/gray environments — the grade matches the light
- Cold/clinical mood — the lack of color warmth feels detached
- Gritty/realistic subject matter — war, crime, poverty
- Green or blue tint — desaturation plus a single color push

**Conflicts with**:
- Vibrant/energetic mood — too drained
- Warm/inviting feeling — desaturation removes warmth
- Fashion/beauty — too muted for most commercial use

**Example combination**: Desaturated + slight green tint + overcast + urban environment → Fincher-esque clinical mood, colors present but suppressed, everything feels slightly wrong

**Prompt cues**: desaturated muted color grade; drained low-saturation color; cold green-grey tint

---

### Warm Vintage / Faded

**What it is**: Lifted shadows (blacks don't go fully black, they fade to dark brown/gray), warm overall tint, reduced contrast, slightly desaturated midtones. The "Instagram filter" look, but also the look of aged or printed photographs.

**Visual effect**: Nostalgic, soft, romantic. The lifted shadows remove harshness. The warm tint adds comfort. The reduced contrast feels gentle. Reads as memory, nostalgia, or a curated past.

**Pairs well with**:
- Lifestyle/wedding photography — the warmth and softness flatter
- Golden hour — warm grade + warm light compounds the warmth
- Film stock references (Portra, Gold) — the warm vintage grade mimics film characteristics
- Soft/diffused light — the grade's gentleness matches soft light

**Conflicts with**:
- Crisp/modern rendering — too soft and warm
- High contrast drama — the lifted shadows remove it
- Cool/clinical tones — too warm

**Prompt cues**: warm faded vintage color grade; lifted milky shadows; amber tint; low contrast

---

### Bleach Bypass / Silver Retention

**What it is**: A photochemical technique (or digital emulation) where the silver in the film isn't fully removed during processing, leaving metallic silver over the color dye. Originally a lab technique, now commonly emulated digitally.

**Visual effect**: Reduced saturation with increased contrast — colors are muted but blacks are deep and highlights are bright. Slightly metallic, desaturated, gritty quality. The image feels simultaneously high-contrast and low-color. Used in Saving Private Ryan, Minority Report, and many war/action films.

**Pairs well with**:
- War/combat imagery — the gritty desaturation is genre-defining
- Action/thriller — high contrast + low saturation = tension
- Hard/directional lighting — the contrast amplifies dramatic light
- Gritty realism — the metallic quality feels raw

**Conflicts with**:
- Vivid color — bleach bypass suppresses it
- Soft/romantic mood — too harsh and contrasty
- Beauty/fashion — too desaturated and gritty

**Example combination**: Bleach bypass + hard side light + war scene + desaturated → deep blacks, muted colors, metallic sheen on skin, high contrast, Saving Private Ryan intensity

**Prompt cues**: bleach bypass look; desaturated color with harsh contrast; deep blacks and bright highlights; gritty metallic sheen

---

### Day for Night

**What it is**: A technique of shooting in daylight and grading the footage to appear as nighttime — heavy blue tint, crushed blacks, reduced exposure.

**Visual effect**: An artificial nighttime look with an obviously stylized quality. The blue tint is deeper than real night. Shadows are exaggerated. Some detail remains visible that would be lost in actual darkness. Reads as theatrical or fantastical rather than realistically nocturnal.

**Pairs well with**:
- Fantasy/stylized scenes — the obvious artifice works for non-realistic content
- Deep blue color grading — the defining color
- Moonlit exterior scenes — the intended simulation

**Conflicts with**:
- Realistic nighttime — real night is darker and less uniformly blue
- Warm tones — day for night is exclusively cool/blue
- Interior scenes — the technique is designed for exteriors

**Prompt cues**: day for night grade; deep blue tint over the scene; exaggerated dark shadows; stylized moonlit look

---

### Cross-Processed Look

**What it is**: Developing film in the wrong chemistry on purpose — typically slide film (E-6) in C-41 negative chemistry, or negative film in E-6 — or its digital emulation. The mismatch shifts hues, boosts saturation and casts unnatural color: typically greens shift yellow, shadows go teal, highlights go warm.

**Visual effect**: Punchy, contrasty, color-shifted. The colors feel "wrong" in an energetic way. Greens go sickly, blues go cyan, skin tones shift toward yellow or magenta; the exact shift depends on the stock. The 1990s fashion photography look (Nick Knight, Anton Corbijn).

**Pairs well with**:
- Fashion editorial — the shifted colors are deliberately stylized
- 1990s aesthetic — the era's defining look
- High saturation scenes — amplifies the weirdness
- Punchy/editorial energy — reads as confident and stylish
- Experimental/art — the unpredictability is creative

**Conflicts with**:
- Natural color accuracy — the opposite
- Subtle/quiet mood — cross-process is loud
- Warm/cozy tones — the color shifts are unpredictable
- Predictable results — each stock and chemistry pairing shifts differently

**Prompt cues**: cross-processed color; shifted hues, yellow greens and cyan blues; punchy contrast; magenta and yellow color casts

---

### Technicolor (Three-Strip)

**What it is**: The specific hyper-saturated color process of Golden Age Hollywood (1930s-1960s). Three strips of film captured red, green, blue separately, producing impossibly rich, slightly artificial color. Wizard of Oz, Singin' in the Rain, Gone with the Wind.

**Visual effect**: Extremely saturated reds and greens. Rich skin tones (slightly ruddy). Deep blacks. A "painted" quality to color — vivid but not harsh. Everything looks slightly more real than real. The warmth and richness are distinctive and instantly recognizable as "old Hollywood color."

**Pairs well with**:
- Period costume — the era of Technicolor was the era of glamour
- Theatrical lighting — bold production design
- Bold production design — saturated sets and costumes
- Glamour portraiture — the Hollywood look

**Conflicts with**:
- Natural/documentary — Technicolor is deliberately artificial
- Desaturated/muted — the opposite approach
- Modern muted grading — Technicolor is vivid
- Gritty realism — too beautiful

**Prompt cues**: three-strip vintage film color; extremely saturated reds and greens; deep rich blacks; painted-looking vivid color

---

### HDR / Tone-Mapped Look

**What it is**: The over-processed look from aggressive tone mapping of HDR bracketed exposures. Equalizes all luminance — shadows and highlights both fully visible. The "real estate listing photo" aesthetic.

**Visual effect**: Halos around edges, hyper-detailed textures, flat-feeling despite visible contrast. Slightly surreal — the eye sees detail everywhere simultaneously, which feels unnatural. The specific "Flickr HDR circa 2010" quality.

**Pairs well with**:
- Architecture/real estate — the native format
- Landscapes (intentional) — dramatic sky + detailed foreground
- Surrealist imagery — the unnatural detail is dreamlike
- Tilt-shift — miniature effect + HDR detail is a combination

**Conflicts with**:
- Naturalism — HDR tone mapping is obviously processed
- Portraiture — makes skin look terrible
- Cinematic looks — too flat and detailed
- Elegant/refined aesthetics — too crunchy

**Prompt cues**: HDR tone-mapped look; halos around edges; hyper-detailed textures everywhere; flat surreal contrast

---

### Matte Painting Color

**What it is**: The color quality of traditional hand-painted matte paintings used in classic film VFX. Slightly simplified value range, paint-like blending, subtly artificial but beautiful. Original Star Wars backgrounds, classic Disney mattes.

**Visual effect**: Slightly hazy atmospheric perspective. Simplified palette compared to reality. Soft edges in distant elements. "Painted reality" quality — real enough to believe but with the warmth and simplification of painted work.

**Pairs well with**:
- Fantasy landscapes — the epic vista
- Golden hour lighting — warm, atmospheric
- Classic cinema aesthetic — the era of physical matte paintings
- Illustrated realism — the bridge between photo and painting

**Conflicts with**:
- Photorealism — the painterly simplification gives it away
- Harsh direct lighting — mattes are atmospheric
- Modern CGI look — different visual quality entirely
- Documentary — too polished and idealized

**Prompt cues**: matte painting look; hazy atmospheric depth; simplified painted color; soft distant edges

---

### Negative Scan Look

**What it is**: The specific aesthetic of scanning color negative film, including process artifacts. Orange mask warm cast, lifted blacks, and the character of different scanners.

**Visual effect**: **Frontier scan** — punchier contrast, more saturated, slightly cooler. "Instagram before Instagram." **Noritsu scan** — warmer, softer, more muted contrast. Skin tones warmer and more flattering. Both share: slightly lifted blacks (shadows don't reach true black), organic warmth, slight imperfection.

**Pairs well with**:
- Lifestyle/portrait photography — the warm organic quality
- Wedding photography — the romantic warmth
- Street photography — authentic analog feel
- Warm natural light — the scan character amplifies warmth

**Conflicts with**:
- Clinical precision — the scan adds character/imperfection
- Digital sharpness — analog scans have inherent softness
- Futuristic aesthetics — too analog and warm
- High contrast B&W — different process entirely

**Prompt cues**: scanned film negative look; lifted blacks that never reach true black; warm organic tones; soft film imperfection

## Tonal Treatments

### Black and White

**What it is**: Complete removal of color information. The image exists only as values of light and dark. The oldest and most fundamental photographic treatment.

**Visual effect**: Abstracts from reality. Removes the distraction of color, emphasizing form, texture, contrast, light, and composition. Emotions feel heightened because the viewer processes the image differently — it's already one step removed from reality. Reads as serious, artistic, timeless, or dramatic.

**Pairs well with**:
- Hard directional light — B&W amplifies the contrast and shadow drama
- Strong textures — grain, skin texture, fabric, stone all read powerfully in monochrome
- High contrast — deep blacks + bright whites = graphic impact
- Portraiture — the emphasis on form and light over color is flattering and expressive
- Film grain — B&W grain has an iconic quality

**Conflicts with**:
- Color-dependent subjects — fashion where the clothing color matters, food photography
- Warm/cool mood distinctions — B&W removes temperature
- Subtle color grading — there's no color to grade

**Example combination**: Black and white + high contrast + hard side light + portrait + visible grain → dramatic, timeless, textural, form and light doing all the work

**Prompt cues**: black and white image; rich greyscale tones; strong light and shadow contrast

---

### Sepia Tone

**What it is**: B&W image toned warm brown, named after cuttlefish ink pigment. Originally a chemical darkroom process that also improved print longevity. Now universally associated with antique photographs.

**Visual effect**: Warm, aged, antique. The brown tone adds warmth to monochrome without introducing full color. Reads as historical, nostalgic, or deliberately vintage. The association with old photographs is so strong that sepia immediately signals "the past."

**Pairs well with**:
- Period/historical subjects — the aging effect matches
- Soft/warm lighting — reinforces the warmth
- Portraiture — warm-toned monochrome is flattering
- Vintage texture (grain, scratches, vignetting) — the full aged-photo package

**Conflicts with**:
- Modern/contemporary subjects — sepia looks backward, not forward
- Cool mood — sepia is inherently warm
- Vivid/energetic feeling — sepia is quiet and reflective

**Prompt cues**: sepia toned photograph; warm brown monochrome; aged antique print look

---

### Cyanotype (Blue Tone)

**What it is**: Monochrome image toned in Prussian blue. Originally an iron-salt photographic printing process invented in 1842 (the "blueprint" is a cyanotype). The distinctive deep blue with white highlights.

**Visual effect**: Cool, ethereal, otherworldly. The blue tone creates a dreamlike or melancholic quality. Reads as historical (but different from sepia — industrial/scientific rather than portrait/domestic), artistic, or deliberately stylized. The blue-and-white palette is striking and graphic.

**Pairs well with**:
- Botanical/nature subjects — the original cyanotypes were botanical prints
- Cool/melancholic mood — the blue is inherently cold
- Graphic compositions — the high-contrast blue and white is bold
- Historical/artistic reference — signals knowledge of photographic history

**Conflicts with**:
- Warm mood — cyanotype is cold
- Realistic rendering — the blue is obviously stylized
- Subtle/understated treatment — the blue is strong

**Prompt cues**: cyanotype print; deep Prussian blue and white; blueprint-like monochrome

---

### Selenium Tone

**What it is**: B&W print toned with selenium, producing a blue-black or slightly purple-cool shift in the shadows. More subtle than sepia or cyanotype. Also increases print archival stability.

**Visual effect**: Cool-neutral, rich, deep. The shadows gain a subtle blue-black quality that adds depth without an obvious color cast. The most "serious" and subtle of the toning methods. Used in fine art printing for its archival benefits and its slight cool richness.

**Pairs well with**:
- Fine art B&W — the subtle tone adds depth without distraction
- Rich shadow detail — the selenium shift lives in the darks
- High-quality printing — the archival association signals craft
- Cool/neutral mood — subtly cool without being as obvious as cyanotype

**Conflicts with**:
- Warm tones — selenium is cool
- Obvious stylization — the effect is subtle, not dramatic
- Casual/snapshot aesthetic — too refined

**Prompt cues**: selenium toned black and white; cool blue-black shadows; rich deep neutral tones

---

### Duotone

**What it is**: Two-color image — a monochrome base with a second color layered over specific tonal ranges. Black + a color (blue, red, gold, etc.) is most common. Originally a printing technique, now a design/photography treatment.

**Visual effect**: Graphic, bold, stylized. The two-color restriction creates a strong visual identity. Different color pairings produce different moods — black + gold feels luxurious, black + red feels intense, black + teal feels modern. The technique bridges B&W and full color — more expressive than monochrome, more controlled than full color.

**Pairs well with**:
- Graphic design / poster aesthetic — duotone is a design tool
- Bold/high-contrast compositions — the restriction demands strong form
- Brand identity — the two-color scheme can match brand colors
- Editorial/magazine layout — graphic and eye-catching
- Portraits — the two-color treatment is striking on faces

**Conflicts with**:
- Natural/realistic color — duotone is obviously artificial
- Subtle tonal work — duotone is bold, not subtle
- Complex multi-color scenes — the restriction eliminates variety

**Example combination**: Duotone (black + electric blue) + high contrast portrait + hard light → graphic, modern, bold, face rendered in deep black shadows and vivid blue highlights

**Prompt cues**: duotone image; two-color print; shadows in one ink, highlights in another; bold graphic color

---

### High Saturation

**What it is**: Colors pushed to maximum vibrancy. Every hue is as intense as possible.

**Visual effect**: Vivid, energetic, overwhelming. The image buzzes with color energy. Can feel tropical, psychedelic, pop-art, or commercial depending on the palette. Common in advertising, music videos, and social media content.

**Pairs well with**:
- Bright/direct lighting — saturation needs light to pop
- Bold/graphic compositions — the intensity matches visual boldness
- Fashion with colorful clothing — each piece screams its color
- Tropical/summer scenes — bright sun + saturated = vacation energy
- Pop art / Warhol-esque treatment — hyper-saturation is pop art

**Conflicts with**:
- Subtle/quiet mood — too loud
- Natural/realistic rendering — over-saturation is obviously processed
- Skin tone accuracy — pushing saturation makes skin look unnatural

**Prompt cues**: highly saturated colors; vivid intense hues; color at full strength

---

### Pastel / Low Saturation + High Value

**What it is**: Colors present but softened — low saturation combined with lifted exposure. Pinks, powder blues, soft yellows, lavender. The candy-colored, soft aesthetic.

**Visual effect**: Gentle, dreamy, feminine (in traditional coding), delicate. The soft colors feel like they're seen through gauze. Reads as romantic, youthful, or fashion-editorial in certain contexts. The absence of deep blacks or vivid color creates an airy, floating quality.

**Pairs well with**:
- Soft/diffused light — the light quality matches the color softness
- Fashion/beauty — the pastel palette is commercially ubiquitous
- Overexposed highlights — the blown whites add to the airiness
- Minimal compositions — pastel + negative space = calm
- Spring/floral subjects — pastels are the colors of spring

**Conflicts with**:
- Drama/intensity — too soft
- High contrast — pastels need low contrast to exist
- Gritty/raw aesthetic — the opposite energy

**Example combination**: Pastel palette + soft diffused light + fashion subject + minimal background + slight overexposure → airy, dreamy, soft pink and lavender tones, blown white highlights, editorial calm

**Prompt cues**: soft pastel palette; pale light colors; airy bright image with no deep blacks

---

### Split Toning

**What it is**: Assigning different color hues to highlights and shadows independently. Classic: warm amber highlights + cool blue shadows. A precise tool for mood control.

**Visual effect**: Sophisticated, stylized mood without flattening contrast. The color split creates visual temperature tension — warm where light falls, cool where shadow lives. Reads as editorial, polished, cinematic.

**Pairs well with**:
- Portrait photography — the warm/cool split flatters skin
- Golden hour — amber highlights amplify the warmth naturally
- Film emulation — many film stocks have inherent split toning
- Desaturated base images — split toning works on muted images

**Conflicts with**:
- Heavy overall saturation — fights the subtlety
- Neon/rainbow palettes — too many colors competing
- Monochrome — split toning needs a color-capable image

**Prompt cues**: split toning; warm tinted highlights; cool tinted shadows
