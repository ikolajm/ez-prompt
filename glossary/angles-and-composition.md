# Angles & Composition

Camera angle controls the power dynamic between viewer and subject. Composition controls where the eye goes and how the image feels. Together they determine how the viewer *relates* to the image — who has power, what's important, and where to look.

---

## Camera Angles

### Eye Level

**What it is**: Camera at the subject's eye height, looking straight on. No upward or downward tilt. The default, neutral perspective.

**Visual effect**: Neutral and natural. No psychological weight — the viewer meets the subject as an equal. The "invisible" angle: it doesn't draw attention to itself. The subject appears as they are, with no exaggeration of power or vulnerability.

**Pairs well with**:
- Documentary/naturalistic work — the neutrality reads as honest
- Portraiture — respectful, direct connection with the subject
- Normal focal lengths (50mm) — both are "neutral," the image feels unmanipulated
- Eye contact — the subject meeting the lens at their own level is intimate

**Conflicts with**:
- Dramatic power dynamics — eye level is deliberately flat
- Dynamic/action compositions — too static and even
- Stylized or surreal aesthetics — too grounded in reality

**Example combination**: Eye level + 50mm + natural light + direct gaze → honest, intimate portrait with no optical manipulation, subject and viewer on equal footing

**Prompt cues**: eye-level shot; camera straight on at eye height

---

### Low Angle (Looking Up)

**What it is**: Camera positioned below the subject, tilting upward. The subject looms above the viewer.

**Visual effect**: Makes the subject appear powerful, dominant, imposing, or heroic. The viewer is literally looking up at them. Chin and jawline are emphasized. Background becomes sky or ceiling, simplifying what's behind the subject. Buildings and structures appear to tower. At extreme angles, converging vertical lines create dramatic perspective.

**Pairs well with**:
- Hero/power shots — the angle reinforces dominance
- Architecture — buildings become monumental from below
- Wide angle lens — exaggerates the perspective distortion, makes subject loom even larger
- Strong jawline/bone structure — the angle sculpts from below
- Dramatic lighting from above — raking across the face from an upward viewpoint

**Conflicts with**:
- Vulnerability/intimacy — the subject appears too strong
- Unflattering nostril visibility — extreme low angles show up the nose
- Calm/neutral composition — low angle introduces tension

**Example combination**: Low angle + wide angle + hero subject + dramatic sky behind → figure towering against clouds, converging lines from buildings, sense of power and scale

**Prompt cues**: low angle shot looking up; subject looming large from below; camera near the ground

---

### High Angle (Looking Down)

**What it is**: Camera positioned above the subject, tilting down. The viewer looks down at the subject.

**Visual effect**: Makes the subject appear small, vulnerable, trapped, or childlike. The viewer holds the power. Background becomes the ground, floor, or surface the subject is on. Reduces the sense of a subject's physical stature. Can also read as protective/tender when used gently.

**Pairs well with**:
- Vulnerability/emotional scenes — the subject feels small and exposed
- Children/pets — naturally matches a parent/guardian perspective
- Establishing spatial context — you see the ground/environment around the subject
- Flat lay composition — extreme high angle becomes top-down
- Short telephoto — compression + downward angle isolates the subject against the ground

**Conflicts with**:
- Power/heroism — the angle undermines authority
- Imposing subjects — they'll look diminished
- Horizontal landscapes — looking down compresses the horizon

**Example combination**: High angle + medium shot + subject sitting on floor + soft light → vulnerable, intimate, viewer looking down protectively, subject surrounded by the space around them

**Prompt cues**: high angle shot looking down; subject small below the camera; ground filling the background

---

### Bird's Eye View (Directly Overhead)

**What it is**: Camera directly above the scene, looking straight down. Also called "top-down" or "God's eye" view. The most extreme high angle.

**Visual effect**: Abstracts the scene. People and objects become shapes and patterns on a surface. Removes normal perspective — no converging lines, no vanishing points. Creates a map-like or diagrammatic quality. Establishes spatial relationships between elements.

**Pairs well with**:
- Flat lay / product arrangement — the standard overhead product shot
- Food photography — the plate as composition
- Tilt-shift miniature effect — overhead + selective focus = toy model look
- Pattern/graphic compositions — the scene becomes a design
- Dance/choreography — bodies form patterns from above
- Establishing shots — the geography of a space

**Conflicts with**:
- Facial expression/connection — faces are foreshortened or invisible
- Depth/dimension — the overhead view flattens everything
- Intimate portraiture — too distant and abstract

**Example combination**: Bird's eye + food spread on table + warm natural light from side + saturated color → organized flat lay of dishes, graphic arrangement, textures visible, no depth compression

**Prompt cues**: bird's-eye view; directly overhead, looking straight down; flat map-like layout

---

### Worm's Eye View (From the Ground)

**What it is**: Camera at ground level or below, looking straight up. The most extreme low angle.

**Visual effect**: Maximum distortion of scale. Everything towers. People appear monumental. Trees and buildings converge toward the sky. The viewer feels small, childlike, or insect-like. Unusual and attention-grabbing — this is an angle humans rarely experience.

**Pairs well with**:
- Dramatic/surreal imagery — the unfamiliar viewpoint creates impact
- Architecture — buildings converge to vanishing point overhead
- Forest canopy — trees reaching upward, converging lines
- Fisheye lens — the distortion amplifies the extreme angle
- Action/sports — athletes leaping overhead

**Conflicts with**:
- Naturalistic/documentary — the angle is too unusual to feel candid
- Flattering portraiture — extreme foreshortening of the body
- Calm compositions — the angle is inherently dynamic

**Example combination**: Worm's eye + fisheye + forest canopy + golden hour light filtering through → trees radiating outward from center, curved distortion, light rays between leaves, immersive vertigo

**Prompt cues**: worm's-eye view; camera on the ground looking up; everything towering overhead; lines converging toward the sky

---

### Dutch Angle (Tilted Horizon)

**What it is**: Camera rotated on its lens axis so the horizon line is diagonal. Named from "Deutsch" (German) — originated in German Expressionist cinema of the 1920s. Earliest known use: 1906.

**Visual effect**: Unease, disorientation, instability, madness. The tilted world signals that something is wrong. Vertical lines lean, the ground feels unstable, the viewer feels off-balance. Used extensively in film noir, horror, thrillers, and comic book adaptations (the 1960s Batman series used distinct tilt angles per villain).

**Pairs well with**:
- Horror/thriller mood — the instability amplifies tension
- Film noir — foundational technique of the genre
- Wide angle — the distortion compounds the tilt
- Neon/colored lighting — stylized light + tilted frame = maximum unease
- Candid/chaotic scenes — parties, crowds, action

**Conflicts with**:
- Calm/serene compositions — the tilt introduces anxiety
- Architectural accuracy — all lines are wrong
- Corporate/commercial — reads as untrustworthy or chaotic
- Overuse — loses impact quickly if every shot is tilted

**Example combination**: Dutch angle + wide angle + neon lighting + nighttime urban → tilted buildings, diagonal lines of light, disorienting perspective, thriller energy

**Prompt cues**: dutch angle; tilted horizon; leaning vertical lines

---

### Over the Shoulder (OTS)

**What it is**: Camera positioned behind one subject's shoulder, looking at another subject or scene. The foreground subject's shoulder and/or head is partially visible, framing the background subject.

**Visual effect**: Establishes relationship between two subjects. Creates depth through foreground/background layering. The viewer is positioned "with" the foreground subject, sharing their viewpoint. Reads as conversational, observational, or voyeuristic depending on context.

**Pairs well with**:
- Dialogue scenes — the standard conversational framing
- Depth layering — foreground blur + sharp background subject
- Short telephoto (85-135mm) — compression brings both subjects together visually
- Voyeuristic/surveillance mood — watching from behind someone

**Conflicts with**:
- Single-subject isolation — the foreground element shares the frame
- Wide establishing shots — too intimate and narrow
- Direct eye contact with viewer — the subject faces away from camera or toward the other person

---

### POV (Point of View)

**What it is**: Camera placed where the subject's eyes would be, showing what they see. The viewer becomes the subject.

**Visual effect**: Maximum immersion. The viewer experiences the scene firsthand. Often handheld/shaky to simulate real human movement. Can show hands, tools, or partial body of the subject entering the frame. Creates urgency, intimacy, or horror depending on context.

**Pairs well with**:
- Handheld/shaky movement — simulates human head movement
- Wide angle — approximates human field of view
- Horror/thriller — the viewer can't see what's behind them
- Action/sports (GoPro aesthetic) — immersive first-person experience
- Visible hands/body — grounds the POV in a physical presence

**Conflicts with**:
- Seeing the subject — by definition, they're behind the camera
- Stable/composed framing — POV implies movement and imperfection
- Detached/observational mood — POV is the opposite of detached

**Example combination**: POV + wide angle + handheld shake + running through corridor → first-person urgency, hallway walls streaking past, motion blur, immersive panic

**Prompt cues**: first-person point of view; seen through the viewer's own eyes; handheld framing

## Shot Size / Framing

How much of the subject fills the frame. Each size shifts the balance between subject detail and environmental context.

### Extreme Close-Up (ECU)

**What it is**: A single feature fills the frame — an eye, lips, a hand, a detail. No surrounding context visible.

**Visual effect**: Intense, intimate, confrontational. Forces the viewer into extreme proximity. Reveals detail invisible at normal viewing distance. The subject becomes abstract at this scale — an eye becomes a landscape of iris texture and reflected light.

**Pairs well with**:
- Macro lens — optical tool designed for this framing
- Beauty/skin detail — pores, freckles, lashes become the subject
- Tension/horror — extreme closeness creates discomfort
- Texture/material studies — surface becomes the image
- Shallow depth of field — only a sliver is sharp at this distance

**Conflicts with**:
- Context/storytelling — you can't see anything beyond the detail
- Recognizable subjects — the person becomes unidentifiable
- Wide/establishing compositions — opposite intent

**Example combination**: ECU of eye + macro lens + ring light catchlight + saturated iris color → iris texture fills the frame, circular catchlight, individual lashes, abstract beauty

**Prompt cues**: extreme close-up; a single detail fills the frame; edges cropped tight

---

### Close-Up (CU)

**What it is**: Face fills the frame, roughly from forehead to chin. The standard portrait framing for emotional intensity.

**Visual effect**: Intimate, emotional, direct. Facial expression dominates — every micro-expression is readable. Background is minimal or absent. Creates a direct connection between subject and viewer. The workhorse of cinematic emotional storytelling.

**Pairs well with**:
- Portrait lenses (85-135mm) — flattering compression at this framing
- Shallow depth of field — background dissolves, face is everything
- Emotional/dramatic scenes — the face carries the story
- Rembrandt or butterfly lighting — sculpts the face at this proximity
- Direct eye contact — the closeness amplifies the connection

**Conflicts with**:
- Environmental context — you lose everything beyond the face
- Body language/gesture — invisible at this framing
- Wide-angle lenses — unflattering distortion at close range

**Example combination**: Close-up + 85mm f/1.4 + Rembrandt lighting + direct gaze → face fills frame, one side in shadow with triangle, eyes sharp against melted background, emotional intensity

**Prompt cues**: close-up shot; subject fills the frame; minimal background

---

### Medium Close-Up (MCU)

**What it is**: Head and shoulders. The standard "talking head" framing — interviews, news anchors, corporate headshots.

**Visual effect**: Conversational distance. Close enough to read expression, far enough to include body language of the shoulders and upper body. Natural, comfortable, professional.

**Pairs well with**:
- Corporate/editorial headshots — the standard framing
- Three-point lighting — designed for this shot size
- Interview setups — conversational and professional
- Moderate depth of field (f/2.8-f/4) — subject sharp, background softly blurred

**Conflicts with**:
- Extreme emotion — too much distance for raw intensity
- Full body/fashion — can't see the outfit
- Environmental storytelling — too tight for context

**Prompt cues**: medium close-up; framed from the chest up

---

### Medium Shot (MS)

**What it is**: Subject framed from roughly the waist up. The most versatile and commonly used framing. Also called a "mid shot."

**Visual effect**: Balanced — shows facial expression AND body language/gesture. Enough background to establish basic context without overwhelming the subject. Natural viewing distance. The "default" framing that most closely approximates how we see people in everyday conversation.

**Pairs well with**:
- Normal to short telephoto (50-85mm) — natural perspective at this distance
- Environmental portraits — subject + context balanced
- Fashion from the waist up — clothing visible with face detail
- Moderate depth of field — background present but not competing
- Three-point or natural lighting — the standard studio shot

**Conflicts with**:
- Extreme intimacy — too distant for raw emotion
- Full outfit/fashion — can't see below the waist
- Wide landscapes — too much subject, not enough environment

**Prompt cues**: medium shot; framed from the waist up; background visible for context

---

### Full Shot / Long Shot (LS)

**What it is**: Subject's entire body visible in the frame, with some surrounding environment. Head to toe with room to breathe.

**Visual effect**: Establishes the subject within their environment. Body language, posture, outfit, and stance are fully visible. The viewer sees the person in context — where they are matters as much as who they are. Fashion photography lives here.

**Pairs well with**:
- Fashion/editorial — the full outfit is the point
- Environmental storytelling — subject and setting together
- Wide angle (28-35mm) — captures both subject and surroundings
- Architecture/interiors — person within a space
- Full body language — posture, stance, gesture all readable

**Conflicts with**:
- Facial detail — too distant for expression to carry
- Intimate emotion — the distance creates detachment
- Shallow depth of field isolation — harder to separate subject from environment at this distance

**Example combination**: Full shot + 35mm + architectural interior + natural window light → full body visible, outfit and posture clear, room geometry provides context, person within the space

**Prompt cues**: full shot; whole subject head to toe; subject within its surroundings

---

### Wide Shot / Extreme Long Shot (ELS)

**What it is**: Subject is small within a large environment. The setting dominates. The person is context, not the focus.

**Visual effect**: Establishes scale, location, and atmosphere. The subject is dwarfed by the environment. Creates a sense of vastness, isolation, or epic scale. The landscape/architecture/setting is the real subject.

**Pairs well with**:
- Landscape photography — the classic use
- Establishing shots — "this is where we are"
- Wide and ultra-wide lenses — captures the expansive environment
- Deep depth of field (f/8-f/16) — everything sharp front to back
- Sense of isolation/solitude — tiny figure in vast space

**Conflicts with**:
- Character/expression — the person is too small to read
- Intimate connection — maximum distance between viewer and subject
- Product/detail work — too far away for specifics

**Example combination**: Extreme long shot + ultra-wide + lone figure on beach + overcast sky → vast empty beach, tiny silhouette, muted color, overwhelming sense of space and solitude

**Prompt cues**: wide shot; tiny subject in a vast setting; setting dominates the frame

## Composition Principles

Rules and techniques for arranging elements within the frame. These are about *where* things go and *how* the eye moves.

### Rule of Thirds

**What it is**: The frame divided into a 3x3 grid by two horizontal and two vertical lines. Key subjects placed along the lines or at their four intersection points ("power points"). First written down by John Thomas Smith in 1797.

**Visual effect**: Creates dynamic, off-center balance. Prevents the static quality of dead-center subjects. Adds tension and energy. The most widely used composition rule — images that follow it feel "right" to most viewers without them knowing why.

**Pairs well with**:
- Horizon placement — horizon on upper or lower third line, never center
- Portrait eye placement — eyes on upper third line
- Moving subjects — extra space in the direction of movement
- Any focal length — universally applicable
- Landscape — foreground interest on lower third, sky on upper

**Conflicts with**:
- Centered symmetry — rule of thirds pulls things off center
- Extreme minimalism — sometimes dead center is the right choice
- Formal/geometric compositions — symmetry has its own power

**Prompt cues**: rule of thirds composition; subject placed off-center

---

### Center Composition / Symmetry

**What it is**: Subject placed dead center, often with symmetrical framing. The opposite of rule of thirds. Deliberate, formal, confrontational.

**Visual effect**: Static, powerful, formal. The subject dominates and the viewer has nowhere else to look. Symmetry creates a sense of order, perfection, control. When the environment is also symmetrical (hallways, architecture, reflections), the effect is amplified. Wes Anderson built an entire visual identity on centered symmetry.

**Pairs well with**:
- Symmetrical environments — hallways, architecture, doorways, reflections
- Direct eye contact — centered subject staring into the lens is confrontational
- Normal to short telephoto lens — neutral perspective reinforces the formal quality
- Geometric/architectural subjects — symmetry in the environment matches the framing
- Formal/deliberate mood — reads as intentional and controlled

**Conflicts with**:
- Dynamic/energetic compositions — center is inherently static
- Rule of thirds — directly opposing principles
- Candid/naturalistic work — centering feels posed

**Example combination**: Center composition + symmetrical hallway + single figure walking toward camera + normal lens → formal geometry, figure centered in converging lines, Wes Anderson order

**Prompt cues**: centered symmetrical composition; subject dead center; mirrored balance on both sides

---

### Leading Lines

**What it is**: Lines within the scene — roads, fences, rivers, shadows, architectural elements — that guide the viewer's eye toward the subject or through the composition.

**Visual effect**: Directs attention, creates depth, adds movement. The eye follows lines instinctively. Converging lines create perspective depth. Diagonal lines add energy. Curved lines add grace. Leading lines transform a flat image into a spatial experience.

**Pairs well with**:
- Wide angle lenses — exaggerate converging perspective lines
- Rule of thirds — lines leading to a power point
- Deep depth of field — lines remain sharp throughout the frame
- Roads, paths, railway tracks — natural leading line subjects
- Architecture — geometric lines are everywhere

**Conflicts with**:
- Shallow depth of field — lines blur and lose their guiding power
- Cluttered compositions — competing lines confuse the eye
- Centered/static compositions — leading lines imply movement

**Example combination**: Leading lines (road vanishing to horizon) + wide angle + deep depth of field + lone figure at vanishing point → road converges toward distant figure, strong sense of depth and journey

**Prompt cues**: leading lines; lines converging toward the subject

---

### Negative Space

**What it is**: Intentionally empty areas of the composition surrounding the subject. The space where "nothing" is — blank sky, empty wall, open water.

**Visual effect**: Creates breathing room, emphasis, and isolation. The subject becomes more prominent through contrast with the void around it. Reads as minimal, calm, contemplative, or lonely depending on context. The emptiness carries emotional weight.

**Pairs well with**:
- Minimalist aesthetic — negative space IS minimalism
- Isolated subjects — one subject, vast emptiness
- Off-center placement (rule of thirds) — subject in one third, negative space in the other two
- Muted/simple backgrounds — plain sky, solid wall, smooth water
- Typography/design — negative space is where text lives in editorial layout

**Conflicts with**:
- Busy/detailed compositions — clutter fills the negative space
- Tight cropping — no room for emptiness
- Action/energy — negative space is calm and still

**Example combination**: Negative space + single subject in lower-right third + open sky filling 2/3 of frame + muted blue → vast emptiness above, subject small but emphasized, sense of solitude and space

**Prompt cues**: negative space; small subject in a large empty area; minimal clean background

---

### Framing Within the Frame

**What it is**: Using elements in the scene — doorways, windows, arches, tree branches, shadows — to create a frame around the subject within the photograph's own frame.

**Visual effect**: Adds depth by creating foreground/background layers. Draws the eye to the subject through a visual funnel. Creates a voyeuristic quality — looking through something at someone. Adds context about the environment while maintaining focus on the subject.

**Pairs well with**:
- Architecture — doorways, windows, arches are natural frames
- Shallow depth of field — foreground frame blurred, subject sharp
- Portraits — framing the face through hands, objects, or environment
- Over-the-shoulder shots — the foreground person frames the background subject
- Telephoto compression — brings the frame and subject closer together visually

**Conflicts with**:
- Minimalism — framing elements add visual complexity
- Wide open compositions — the frame adds enclosure
- Full negative space — the frame fills it

**Example combination**: Subject framed through doorway + 85mm + shallow depth of field + door frame slightly blurred in foreground → layers of depth, subject sharp in the middle distance, voyeuristic intimacy

**Prompt cues**: frame within a frame; subject seen through a foreground opening

---

### Diagonal Composition

**What it is**: Key elements arranged along a diagonal rather than horizontal/vertical. The subject, lines, or visual weight run corner to corner or at an angle.

**Visual effect**: Dynamic, energetic, unstable. Diagonals create movement and tension where horizontals and verticals create stability. The eye travels along the diagonal, creating a sense of action or momentum. Related to but distinct from Dutch angle — diagonal composition uses elements within a level frame, Dutch angle tilts the frame itself.

**Pairs well with**:
- Action/movement — diagonals reinforce motion
- Dynamic subjects — running, jumping, falling
- Leading lines on the diagonal — roads, stairs at an angle
- Wide angle — the perspective distortion naturally creates diagonals
- Fashion/editorial — energy and attitude

**Conflicts with**:
- Calm/serene compositions — diagonals introduce tension
- Formal symmetry — diagonals break symmetry
- Static/contemplative mood — too energetic

**Prompt cues**: diagonal composition; strong diagonal lines across a level frame

---

### Rule of Odds

**What it is**: Compositions with odd numbers of subjects (3, 5, 7) feel more natural and dynamic than even numbers. Even numbers create symmetrical pairs that feel static; odd numbers create a natural visual hierarchy.

**Visual effect**: Unconscious sense of naturalness and visual interest. Three subjects create a triangle. Five create an asymmetric rhythm. The eye keeps moving because the group can't be evenly split.

**Pairs well with**:
- Group compositions — three people, five objects
- Still life — odd-numbered arrangements of objects
- Repeating elements — three windows, five pillars, seven steps

**Conflicts with**:
- Perfect symmetry — even numbers are needed for mirrored balance
- Pairs/couples — two subjects is inherently even
- Minimalism with a single subject — only one element, the rule doesn't apply

---

### Foreground Interest

**What it is**: Placing an element in the near foreground of the composition to add depth and lead the eye into the scene. Rocks in front of a waterfall, flowers below a mountain, a hand reaching toward the camera.

**Visual effect**: Creates a strong sense of three-dimensional depth. The eye travels from the near foreground through the middle ground to the background — the image has layers. Especially powerful with wide-angle lenses that exaggerate near/far size differences.

**Pairs well with**:
- Wide angle — foreground elements loom large, adding drama
- Deep depth of field (f/8-f/16) — everything sharp from foreground to background
- Landscape — the classic use: flowers/rocks in front, mountains behind
- Leading lines — the foreground element can guide the eye deeper

**Conflicts with**:
- Shallow depth of field — foreground blurs out
- Telephoto compression — near and far appear the same size
- Minimal compositions — foreground adds complexity

**Example combination**: Wide angle + f/11 + wildflowers in foreground + mountains in background + golden hour → foreground flowers large and detailed, mountains sharp in distance, extreme depth, golden light raking across both layers

**Prompt cues**: strong foreground element; layered foreground, middle ground and background

---

### Tight Crop / Unconventional Framing

**What it is**: Deliberately cropping into the subject in unexpected ways — cutting off the top of the head, slicing at the joints, framing only half the face, limbs exiting the frame.

**Visual effect**: Tension, intimacy, and edge. The viewer feels like they're too close or seeing something they shouldn't. Breaks the comfortable "rules" of traditional framing. Reads as modern, editorial, fashion-forward, or intentionally confrontational.

**Pairs well with**:
- Fashion editorial — unconventional framing signals avant-garde
- Close-up and ECU — pushing further into the subject
- Direct flash — raw energy + tight crop = maximum intensity
- High contrast — the boldness matches the aggressive framing
- Disposable/snapshot aesthetic — unintentional-looking crops feel candid

**Conflicts with**:
- Traditional/formal composition — tight crops break the rules
- Full context/storytelling — too close for narrative
- Corporate/commercial — reads as too edgy

**Example combination**: Tight crop through forehead + 35mm at close range + direct flash + high contrast → top of head cut off, face fills frame with slight wide-angle distortion, raw flash, editorial aggression

**Prompt cues**: tight unconventional crop; subject cut off at the frame edges; uncomfortably close framing
